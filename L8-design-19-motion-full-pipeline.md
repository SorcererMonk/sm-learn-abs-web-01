> **Context stamp (September 2026):** the tool map has shifted in your favour. **Cavalry** (procedural 2D motion) became **completely free for individuals**, including commercial work, in **April 2026** (Canva owns it; studios need a Canva Enterprise plan). **DaVinci Resolve** (editing, Fusion compositing, colour grading, Fairlight audio) remains free. **Blender** (5.2 LTS in 2026) covers 3D and 2D (Grease Pencil) animation for free. **After Effects** (the 26.x line from January 2026) added native parametric 3D meshes, Substance materials, an advanced 3D renderer with depth of field, variable-font axis animation, native SVG import and a Curl Noise effect; it's still the industry's lingua franca. **Figma Motion** (open beta, June 2026) now handles UI-scale motion inside the design tool. **The motion-pipeline learning loop:**
1. **Start every piece on paper.** Thumbnails and a written idea before any software. The tool can't tell you what the piece is about.
2. **Styleframes before animation.** If a single frame isn't beautiful, animating it won't help.
3. **Animatic before polish.** Rough timing to the audio, reviewed, before you spend hours on curves.
4. **Block, then spline, then polish.** Never polish a shot whose timing isn't approved.
5. **Study one master frame by frame each week.** Titles from Art of the Title, a Buck or Oddfellows film, a Ben Marriott breakdown.
6. **Ship short pieces often.** A 7-second study with one idea and three colours teaches more than a stalled 2-minute epic (`TD-08#3 · @yuk.aji · Dcs-MiOpO2V`).

## PART 0 — Foundations: What Motion Design Is [Core]

- **Definition and scope:** motion design (motion graphics) is designed movement of graphic elements (type, shapes, illustration, photography, 3D) in time, usually with sound, to communicate. It differs from **character animation** (performance and acting), **VFX** (photoreal integration into live action) and **UI motion** (functional interface movement), though it borrows from all three. The label matters commercially: call yourself an animator and clients imagine cartoons; call yourself a motion designer and they imagine brand and product work (`TD-08#9 · @mikevdvisuals · DcN7g4FtVGg`).
- **What motion designers make:**
  - **Brand and identity motion:** logo animations, brand films, motion guidelines (10 Part 4).
  - **Explainers and product films:** features, launches, onboarding videos, the SaaS and fintech staple.
  - **Title sequences and broadcast:** film and series titles, channel idents, OTT promos, sports graphics.
  - **Social and advertising:** Reels, Shorts, stories, bumpers (6-second ads), animated posts.
  - **Kinetic typography:** type as the main actor, for lyric videos, manifestos and campaigns.
  - **Data and information motion:** animated charts and explainers (22).
  - **Experimental and art work:** personal studies, audio-reactive visuals, generative pieces, installations.
  - **Product and UI films:** device mockups, feature demos, app-store previews (bridging 18).
- **The pipeline at a glance:** **brief → concept → script/VO → moodboard → storyboard → styleframes → animatic → design boards → animation (block → spline → polish) → compositing and 3D → sound design and mix → grade → review and revisions → delivery and archive.** Small pieces compress steps; big ones add more (casting VO, music composition, render management).
- **The 12 principles still apply** (18 Part 1), with more room for exaggeration, anticipation, follow-through and appeal than UI allows. Graphic motion adds its own grammar: **reveals** (masks, wipes), **transitions** (match cuts, morphs), **camera** (virtual moves, parallax, depth), **rhythm** (cutting and animating to the beat), and **typographic performance**.

**Intuition anchors**
- Motion design is graphic design plus time plus sound. Weak design, weak timing or weak sound will each sink a piece.
- The idea carries the piece. Technique only makes it watchable.

**Mastery checks**
- Distinguish motion design, character animation, VFX and UI motion.
- List eight kinds of motion design work with an example of each.
- Recite the pipeline from brief to archive.

**Analysis**
- Watch three pieces: a title sequence (Art of the Title), a SaaS explainer, and a 10-second social ad. For each, identify the idea in one sentence, the pipeline steps you can infer, and where the craft is concentrated.

**Taste**
- The best motion work has a single clear idea you could describe in a sentence. Complexity serves the idea, never replaces it.

## PART 1 — Concept & Pre-production [Core]

- **The brief:** audience, message, length, formats and aspect ratios, deliverables, deadline, budget, brand guidelines, mandatory elements (logo, legal lines, disclaimers), and success measures. Ask the questions early: vague feedback later is usually a question that wasn't asked at the start (`TD-15#15 · @uiuxmanuel · Db82VC5juMd`).
- **Creative direction first:** high-end work begins with direction, not with a tool. Even flashy 3D web experiences start from a creative idea and a reference set (`TD-08#8 · @jerrythewebdev · DcJqTFFRbLc`).
- **The idea:** a one-sentence concept ("A certificate folds itself into an origami elephant", `TD-02#31 · @_saber_.47 · DbAQLHGt3pZ`: personalisation plus an unexpected physical metaphor). Test it: can you explain it in one sentence, and is it specific to this client?
- **Script and voice-over:** for explainers, the script is the spine. Write for the ear: short sentences, one idea per line, about **130–150 words per minute** of spoken English (slower for Hindi and for complex ideas). A 60-second explainer has room for roughly 140 words, which is less than most clients expect.
- **Moodboards and references:** collect frames, colour, type, textures, motion references and music. Give each reference a label saying what you're taking from it (the lighting, the transition, the type treatment). Visual-similarity search (same.energy) is useful when tags fail (`TD-10#58 · @jason_swet · C-2jQjuxvaV`).
- **Storyboards:** thumbnail sketches of key moments, shot by shot, with notes on motion, camera, transitions and timing. Stick figures are fine. Storyboards are where you solve **what happens and in what order**, cheaply.
- **Styleframes:** fully designed key frames (usually 3–6) at final quality: type, colour, composition, texture, lighting. They sell the look to the client and set the standard for every frame after. Clients approve styleframes before animation begins.
- **Design boards:** the full set of designed frames for every scene, especially for complex pieces, so animation becomes execution rather than design-under-pressure.
- **Animatics:** storyboards or styleframes timed to the voice-over and music, with rough moves. The animatic locks **timing and pacing** before expensive animation. Most revisions should happen here.
- **Pitch decks and treatments:** for bigger jobs, a written treatment (idea, tone, visual approach, references, styleframes) sells the direction. Presenting three distinct directions from one brief gives clients a real choice (`TD-02#49 · @ayzz.thedesigner · DM-WQhbNeQX`).

- **A worked example (EcoTrace, 45 seconds):**
  - **One-sentence idea:** "A small factory's tangle of paper receipts untangles itself into one clean BRSR report."
  - **Treatment (short form):** tone calm and competent, not preachy green; palette of paper whites, ink blue and one signal green; flat vector with paper grain; the paper metaphor carries every scene, so the factory, the data and the report are all folded, stacked or unfolded paper; cuts are match cuts where one sheet becomes the next scene.
  - **Shot list (first four shots):** (1) 0:00–0:05, a desk buried in receipts, VO names the problem; (2) 0:05–0:12, receipts lift and sort themselves into three stacks (Scope 1, 2, 3), push-in; (3) 0:12–0:20, stacks flatten into a dashboard, the one control that matters enlarged; (4) 0:20–0:28, the dashboard folds into a report, a single green check.
  - **Word budget:** 45 seconds at ~140 words per minute is about 100 words of VO, including a pause for the end card.
  - This is the level of detail a client can approve, and it's enough for anyone on the team to start styleframes.

**Intuition anchors**
- Every hour in pre-production saves several in animation.
- If the animatic doesn't work, polish won't save it.
- Clients approve pictures and timing. Show them both before you animate.

**Mastery checks**
- List the contents of a motion brief.
- Explain storyboards, styleframes, design boards and animatics and what each locks.
- Estimate word count for a 30, 60 and 90-second voice-over.
- Write a one-paragraph treatment for a given brief.

**Analysis**
- Take a 60-second product explainer you admire. Reverse-engineer its script (transcribe it), storyboard (screenshot key frames) and animatic timing (log the timecode of each beat). How many words per minute? How many shots?

**Taste**
- Great motion work looks effortless because the thinking was done before the animating.

## PART 2 — Designing for Motion [Core]

- **Design frames that will move:** separate layers (anything that will move independently must be its own layer), name them, keep vector artwork editable, extend backgrounds beyond the frame (overscan) for camera moves, and design **start and end states** for each element, not just the hero frame.
- **Composition across aspect ratios:** 16:9 (YouTube, TV), **9:16** (Reels, Shorts, TikTok, stories), 1:1 and 4:5 (feeds). Design the key idea to survive all the ratios you deliver, with **safe zones** for platform UI overlays (captions, buttons and usernames cover the bottom and right edges of vertical video).
- **Typography for motion** (04 Part 6):
  - **Hold time:** text must stay on screen long enough to read. A rough guide is **about 3 words per second** for comfortable reading, plus a beat. Headlines can be faster; body text should be avoided.
  - **Weight and size:** thin weights and small sizes shimmer and disappear in compressed video and on phones. Use sturdy weights and generous sizes.
  - **Kinetic type:** type that moves with meaning (scale for emphasis, rhythm matched to speech, letters reacting to one another). **Variable fonts** animate weight and width smoothly (now native in After Effects 2026).
  - **Letterforms from non-visual sources** (a sound, a movement) generate shapes and animation ideas you wouldn't draw directly (`TD-03#6 · @studiopatrickaere · DcspTF-MQWA`).
- **Colour and grading** (03; `TD-14`):
  - Colour carries mood and story across a sequence: *Spider-Verse* uses colour theory scene by scene to tell the story (`TD-14#5 · @cinemahow · DcJvcJvCNqV`; `TD-14#14 · @filmschool.dropouts · DLvcEvUPmJh`); directors like Nolan and Chazelle use colour and black-and-white structurally (`TD-14#23 · @movieluts · DB4O7DhiS7S`; `TD-14#28 · @movieluts · C9AMU2bC5QW`).
  - **Grading basics:** balance first (white balance, exposure, contrast), then the look. The **S-curve** adds contrast (`TD-14#26 · @regime · DAZRu0mSr7O`); opposite grade types (teal-orange, bleach bypass, pastel) create different moods (`TD-14#30 · @movieluts · C79h3wZC0Q0`). *The Batman*'s grade is a study in restraint and consistency (`TD-14#25 · @theqazman · DB3Tvd3RvnC`).
  - **Premium gradients** come from overlapping blurred shapes rather than two-stop interpolation, and animate beautifully (`TD-04#15 · @ginyboi · DTwA8e3jRvF`).
- **Constraint as style:** one subject, three colours, one stated aesthetic thesis, seven seconds produces a coherent piece rather than an effects demo (`TD-08#3`). Strong motion styles are usually a small set of rules applied consistently.
- **Illustration and texture for motion:** flat vector (fast, clean), textured and grainy (warmer, harder to animate), cel-shaded 3D, collage and mixed media, photographic. Decide how each style will move before committing, because textures that swim or boil need deliberate handling.

**Intuition anchors**
- If an element will move on its own, it needs its own layer.
- Design for the smallest screen and the fastest scroll: that's where most motion is watched.
- A sequence's colour script is as important as its storyboard.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain overscan, start/end states and layer preparation for animation.
- Give safe-zone rules for vertical social video.
- State a reading-speed guide for on-screen text and three rules of type for motion.
- Explain a colour script and the basics of grading (balance, S-curve, look).

**Analysis**
- Make a colour script for a 30-second piece: a strip of 8 small frames showing how colour changes with the story. Compare it with a film sequence from `TD-14#5` or `#14`.

**Taste**
- The best motion designers are first-rate graphic designers. Your frames should work as posters.

## PART 3 — Animation Craft: Blocking, Splining, Polish [Core → Deep]

- **The three passes** (borrowed from character animation, used everywhere):
  1. **Blocking:** key poses only, on the right frames, with stepped or linear interpolation. Is the timing right? Does the story read? Get approval here.
  2. **Splining:** switch to smooth interpolation, add in-betweens, shape the curves. Motion now flows but is often floaty or mechanical.
  3. **Polish:** refine curves, add overlap, follow-through, anticipation, secondary action, micro-timing, motion blur. This is where "good" becomes "great", and it's only worth doing on approved timing.
- **Keyframes and interpolation:** linear, eased (Bézier), hold (stepped). Most amateur motion is linear or uses the default "easy ease" on everything, which looks generic.
- **The graph editor** (the most important tool in motion craft):
  - **Value graph:** a property's value over time (position, scale, rotation). Its slope is speed; flat means stopped.
  - **Speed graph:** speed over time. A tall, narrow peak is a fast, snappy move; a low, wide curve is gentle.
  - **Pulling influence handles** (After Effects), shaping Bézier handles (Cavalry, Blender, Figma Motion) creates acceleration profiles: a hard ease-out gives impact; a long ease-in gives anticipation.
  - Separate dimensions (X and Y on separate curves) for arcs and more natural paths.
  - Learn to read a curve and predict the motion, and to see a motion and sketch the curve.
- **Spacing and timing:** timing is *when* poses happen; spacing is *how far apart* the in-between positions are. Close spacing is slow; wide spacing is fast. Timing charts (from 2D animation) show this on paper (Richard Williams, *The Animator's Survival Kit*, 2001, is the definitive book).
- **Counting frames** (the habit that separates animators from people who drag keyframes):
  - At **24 fps**, 6 frames is a quarter-second snap, 12 frames a half-second move, 24 frames a full second. At **30 fps**, multiply by 1.25. Learn to *feel* 4, 8, 12 and 24 frames the way a musician feels note values.
  - Rough starting points for graphic moves at 24 fps: a quick pop or scale-in **6–10 frames**, a standard move across frame **12–18**, a heavy object settling **18–30**, a hold long enough to read a short phrase **36–60**. Adjust from there by eye.
  - **On ones vs on twos:** animating "on twos" (a new drawing every second frame at 24 fps, so 12 poses a second) is standard in hand-drawn animation and gives a slightly stepped, tactile feel. Stylised motion work (the *Spider-Verse* look, many 2D shorts) mixes ones and twos deliberately. In After Effects, the Posterize Time effect or a `posterizeTime()` expression does this; Blender has a Stepped Interpolation F-Curve modifier.
  - **Moving holds:** a "hold" that's completely still looks dead on screen. Let a held element drift a few pixels or scale 1–2% over the hold so it stays alive, then accelerate into the next move.
- **Overlap and follow-through in graphics:** offset the start of related layers by a few frames; let parts settle after the main move; stagger letters in a word; let a shape overshoot and settle. These are the small offsets that make graphics feel alive rather than slid.
- **Holds and rhythm:** give the viewer time to read. A move, a hold, a move is more readable than constant motion. Rhythm (alternating fast and slow, busy and calm) keeps attention.
- **Transitions between scenes:** cuts (on the beat or on action), **match cuts** (a shape in scene A becomes a shape in scene B), **morphs**, **wipes and masks**, **camera moves** through the frame, **graphic reveals**, and less common ones like **pixel-block masking used as a transition rather than a filter** (`TD-08#3`). A great transition hides the edit inside an idea.
- **Kinetic typography techniques:** word-by-word or line-by-line reveals synced to VO, scale for emphasis, type as object (it falls, bends, gets pushed), variable-font axis animation, masking type through type, and type locked to 3D camera moves. Keep it readable: every clever move must still leave time to read.
- **Shape and logo animation:** build with strokes (trim paths), shape morphs, and masks; animate a logo's construction or its meaning, not a random spin. Logo animations of 2–5 seconds are a staple commercial deliverable (10 Part 4).
- **Rigging basics:** parenting (children follow parents), null controllers, pin and puppet tools for bending shapes, inverse kinematics for limbs (After Effects's Duik tools, Blender armatures and Grease Pencil rigs, Cavalry's constraints). Rig once, animate many times.
- **Expressions and procedural motion:** small bits of code that drive properties: `wiggle(freq, amp)` for organic randomness, `loopOut()` for cycles, `time * n` for constant rotation, linking one property to another. Cavalry is built around procedural behaviours; Blender has drivers and Geometry Nodes. Your JavaScript skills transfer directly to After Effects expressions.
- **2D character animation (intro):** walk and run cycles, weight, acting and lip-sync are a separate craft. For motion designers, simple characters (limbs as shapes, rubber-hose style) are enough for most explainers. Study Williams, and practise cycles in Blender's Grease Pencil (free) or Krita's animation tools (free).

**Intuition anchors**
- If it looks floaty, the curves are too even. Sharpen the ease-out.
- If it looks mechanical, nothing overlaps. Offset something by two frames.
- Approve timing in blocking, never in polish.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain blocking, splining and polish, and what gets approved at each.
- Read a value graph and a speed graph and describe the motion.
- Explain spacing vs timing.
- Name six scene-transition techniques and when each suits.
- Write three expressions (wiggle, loopOut, a linked property) and explain them.

**Analysis**
- Animate a bouncing ball, then a logo reveal, then a 10-word kinetic-type line, each in three passes, saving a render after each pass. Compare the passes side by side: what did polish add?

**Taste**
- Great animation has contrast in time: fast against slow, move against hold. Uniform motion is the sound of a beginner.

## PART 4 — Compositing, 3D Integration & Effects [Core → Deep]

- **Compositing fundamentals:** layers stacked with **blend modes** (screen, add, multiply, overlay), **masks** and **track mattes** (one layer defines another's visibility, the basis of most reveals), **pre-compositions** (nested sequences) for organisation, **adjustment layers** for effects across layers, **motion blur** matched to shutter angle (180° is the film standard).
- **Tracking and rotoscoping:** 2D point tracking to attach graphics to footage, planar tracking (Mocha) for surfaces like screens and signs, camera tracking for 3D integration, rotoscoping (tracing masks frame by frame, now AI-assisted with Roto Brush or Resolve's Magic Mask) to separate subjects.
- **3D integration:**
  - Render 3D objects in **Blender** (or Cinema 4D) with passes (beauty, shadow, depth, object IDs) and composite in After Effects, Fusion or Cavalry.
  - After Effects's own **Advanced 3D renderer** (2026) now handles meshes, Substance materials, image-based lighting, multi-light shadows and depth of field for simpler 3D inside the comp.
  - A small amount of Blender unlocks a whole aesthetic: chrome objects in Photoshop-composited posters are the classic example (`TD-08#15 · @ctrldesign.jpg · DNbH74mo1b_`: four months of basic Blender on top of Photoshop).
- **Particles and simulation:** particle systems (Cavalry's particles, Blender's particles and Geometry Nodes, paid plug-ins like Trapcode Particular), procedural noise fields (After Effects's Curl Noise effect, 2026), fluids and cloth (Blender). Use them for texture and energy, not as the idea.
- **Looks and finishing:** glow, grain, chromatic aberration, halation, lens distortion, light leaks, vignetting. They unify a piece and hide the "digital" edge. Too much of any is the most common amateur tell.
- **Colour grading:** final grade in **DaVinci Resolve** (free, industry standard) or After Effects's Lumetri. Grade for the whole sequence, not shot by shot (Part 2; `TD-14#24 · @colsipher · DCEB1hjucPO`, `#27 · @mikita.yo · C9NHcPBMEtI` for palette extraction).
- **Rendering and render management:**
  - Render **intermediates** of heavy shots (3D passes, particle simulations, precomps with expensive effects) to image sequences or ProRes and replace the live comp with the render; the final comp then renders fast and predictably.
  - Prefer **image sequences** (PNG, EXR) for long or heavy renders: a crash loses one frame, not the whole file, and you can re-render only the frames that changed.
  - **EXR** stores high-bit-depth, multi-pass data (beauty, depth, normals, object IDs) from Blender for flexible compositing; PNG is fine for 8-bit graphics.
  - Keep a render-time budget per shot and profile the slow ones: motion-blur samples, full-resolution precomps, heavy blurs, and 3D renderers are the usual culprits (38 Part 5).
  - For big jobs, use a render queue overnight (After Effects's Render Queue or Media Encoder, Resolve's Deliver page queue, Blender's command-line rendering), and consider cloud render services for 3D only when the deadline justifies the cost.
- **AI in the pipeline:**
  - **Useful:** ideation frames, background plates, upscaling, rotoscoping and masking, noise reduction, voice clean-up, generated textures, storyboard drafts, and video-to-video style tests.
  - **Risky:** fully generated shots shipped as final work (inconsistent, generic, hard to direct precisely), unclear training-data rights, likeness and voice rights (personality rights in India; 09 Part 2), and platform disclosure rules. Treat AI video tools as a **sketchbook and an assistant**, and keep direction, typography, timing and the idea human (30).

**Intuition anchors**
- Most motion "effects" are compositing: masks, mattes and blend modes used with taste.
- Finish is seasoning. If you can see it, there's probably too much.
- A human-directed idea with modest execution beats a generic generated shot.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain blend modes, track mattes, pre-comps and adjustment layers.
- Explain 2D, planar and camera tracking and when to use each.
- Describe a Blender-to-compositor pipeline with render passes.
- List five AI uses that help and three that create risk.

**Analysis**
- Composite one Blender-rendered object into a photograph or a graphic poster (`TD-08#15`), with shadows and a matched grade. Write down every pass and adjustment you used.

**Taste**
- Seamless compositing is invisible. The viewer should believe the world, not admire the layers.

## PART 5 — Sound, Edit & Rhythm [Core]

- **Sound is half the piece.** Viewers forgive rough animation with great sound more readily than the reverse. Plan sound from the animatic stage (20 covers sound design fully).
- **Animate to sound:** place markers on beats, downbeats and VO phrases; cut and hit key moves on beats (or deliberately just off them for tension); let silence land big moments.
- **Sound design for motion:** whooshes for moves, hits for impacts, risers before reveals, UI clicks for interfaces, ambience for space, Foley for tactility. Layer and mix them under the music and VO, with space for each.
- **Music:** licensed stock (Artlist, Epidemic Sound, Musicbed, Pixabay Music for free options), commissioned music, or sound-logo work. Check the licence covers the platforms, territories and paid ads you need. In India, public performance and broadcast involve bodies like IPRS and PPL; for online brand work, the licence from the music library usually governs.
- **Editing principles:** cut on action or on the beat; J-cuts and L-cuts (audio leading or trailing the picture) for smooth transitions; vary shot lengths to control pace; trim ruthlessly ("kill your darlings", `TD-13#12 · @346eur · DcEA1-lDbgK`).
- **Temp music and stems:** edit the animatic to a temp track only if you're sure you can license it or something like it; otherwise the client falls in love with music you can't use. For the final mix, keep **stems** (separate VO, music, SFX and ambience tracks) so later versions (a 15-second cut, a vertical version, a version with a different VO language) can be re-mixed without starting over. Indian campaigns often need Hindi and regional-language VO versions of one film: plan the animation so line lengths can change (Hindi and Tamil reads often run longer than English), with holds that can stretch.
- **Loudness:** deliver mixes at platform norms: around **−14 LUFS integrated** for most streaming and social platforms, **−23 LUFS** for EU broadcast (EBU R128). Leave true-peak headroom (about −1 dBTP). Check on phone speakers, since that's where most social motion is heard.
- **Captions:** most social video is watched muted at first. Burn in captions or design type that carries the message without sound, and provide caption files for accessibility (15 Part 6).

**Intuition anchors**
- Lock the music or VO before final animation, not after.
- If a cut feels wrong, try moving it two frames toward the beat.
- Design for sound-off first on social, and sound-on for everything else.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain animating to markers and cutting on beats.
- Name six categories of sound design for motion.
- State loudness targets for social/streaming and EU broadcast.
- Explain J- and L-cuts.

**Analysis**
- Take one of your pieces and re-cut it twice: once to a fast track, once to a slow one. Retime the key moves to each. What changed in the piece's meaning?

**Taste**
- Rhythm is what makes motion feel inevitable. Watch great pieces with your eyes closed and you'll hear how much of the timing lives in the sound.

## PART 6 — Delivery: Formats, Specs & Archiving [Core]

- **Frame rates:** **24 fps** (cinematic), **25 fps** (PAL regions, including much Indian broadcast), **30 fps** (web and social), **60 fps** (UI demos, sport, smooth screen recordings). Pick one per project and stay consistent.
- **Resolutions and ratios:** 1920 × 1080 (HD) and 3840 × 2160 (4K) for 16:9; **1080 × 1920 for 9:16** vertical; 1080 × 1080 (1:1); 1080 × 1350 (4:5). Design at the largest you deliver and reframe down.
- **Codecs and containers:**
  - **Masters and intermediates:** **ProRes 422 HQ**, or **ProRes 4444** when you need an alpha channel (transparency); DNxHR as a cross-platform alternative.
  - **Delivery:** **H.264** in MP4 (universal), **H.265/HEVC** (smaller, widely supported on devices), **AV1** (efficient, growing platform support). Upload the highest-quality file the platform accepts; it will re-encode anyway.
  - **Web and product:** WebM/MP4 loops for websites (muted, short, compressed, with a poster image), **Lottie/dotLottie** and **Rive** for vector animation in apps and sites (43), GIF only when nothing else works.
- **Social specifics:** vertical safe zones, the first second as the hook, captions, cover frames, platform duration limits and loudness. Deliver one version per platform rather than stretching one master.
- **Colour management at delivery:**
  - Most video is mastered in **Rec. 709** (the HD standard) and most screens and web browsers assume **sRGB**. They share primaries but differ in how brightness is encoded (gamma), so the same file can look slightly washed-out or too contrasty depending on the player. The classic complaint is the "QuickTime gamma shift", where a render looks lighter in one player than in the editing software.
  - Set your project and render colour settings deliberately (After Effects's colour management settings, Resolve's colour-managed workflows) and **check the final file on the target device**, especially phones, which is where most of your work is seen.
  - **HDR** (Rec. 2100 with PQ or HLG) is common on modern phones and TVs, and platforms such as YouTube accept it. It needs HDR-capable monitoring to grade correctly; for social and product motion, a well-made SDR Rec. 709 master is still the safe default in 2026.
  - Brand colours defined in RGB or HEX for screens may still shift in video compression, especially saturated reds and deep blues. Test a brand-colour frame through the platform's encoder before signing off a campaign.
- **Versioning and organisation:** a folder template (`01_Brief`, `02_Design`, `03_Animation`, `04_Audio`, `05_Renders`, `06_Delivery`), clear file names with version numbers (`brand-film_v03_2026-10-12.aep`), collected projects (After Effects "Collect Files", Resolve archive), and an archive of source files, fonts (licence permitting) and final masters. You'll be asked for "the same film, but 15 seconds and vertical" six months later.

**Intuition anchors**
- Master in the best quality you can store, deliver in the format the platform wants.
- Every platform version is a design task, not an export setting.

**Mastery checks**
- Choose frame rate, resolution, codec and container for six deliverables (TV ident, YouTube explainer, Reel, website hero loop, app animation, transparent overlay).
- Explain when you need ProRes 4444 and why.
- Describe your project folder and naming template.

**Analysis**
- Deliver one 15-second piece in four formats (16:9 H.264, 9:16 H.264 with safe zones, 1:1 with burned-in captions, a transparent ProRes 4444 overlay). Check each on the actual platform or device.

- Clean delivery is part of the craft. A beautiful film with the logo under the Reels "like" button is a failed film.

## PART 7 — Practice & Business of Motion [Core → Deep]

- **Scoping and pricing:** quote by deliverables and complexity (duration, style, 2D/3D, character work, number of versions), not just hours; include **revision rounds** (typically two at styleframe stage, two at animation) and state what counts as a change of scope. Explainer pricing is often quoted per second of finished animation, with large ranges by style and market.
- **The client process:** brief → treatment and styleframes (paid) → animatic → animation drafts → final → delivery. Get approvals in writing at each stage.
- **Spec work and personal projects:** spec pieces for a named brand give you a real subject and shareable work (`TD-15#24 · @diegotomasfx · DUYtjpZjU7r`: a spec Gemini ad built in After Effects), but they're unpaid; choose brands whose visual systems you want to work in, label spec work as unofficial, and avoid implying endorsement. Unofficial brand concepts are an excellent practice format (`TD-02#49`).
- **Showreels and content:** a 60–90 second reel of your best work, strongest first, cut to music; case-study pages with process (storyboards, styleframes, breakdowns). Posting frequent short studies and breakdowns on Instagram and YouTube (including faceless channels that let the work speak) is a legitimate way to get noticed by studios. Full asset-by-asset production breakdowns are among the most instructive and shareable content formats (`TD-02#31`).
- **Where motion designers work:** studios (Buck, Oddfellows, Giant Ant, Ordinary Folk, ManvsMachine, Elastic, Imaginary Forces), in-house brand and product teams (increasingly), agencies, broadcasters and OTT platforms, and freelance. In India, OTT promo teams, ad agencies and studios such as Studio Eeksaurus are active; many Indian motion designers also work remotely for international studios.
- **Rights:** license music, fonts, stock footage and images for the intended use; get releases for recognisable people; check AI tool terms; keep records. Assign copyright in the contract as agreed (09 Part 2 and 10 Part 7 cover commissioning and assignment under the Copyright Act).
- **Freelancing from India for international clients** (practical notes, not legal or tax advice; check with a chartered accountant):
  - Quote in the client's currency where you can, receive through a channel that gives you the inward-remittance paperwork your bank needs (a FIRC or its equivalent), and keep invoices numbered and dated.
  - Under GST, services have a registration threshold of **₹20 lakh** of aggregate turnover in most states (₹10 lakh in some special-category states); exports of services can be **zero-rated**, typically by filing a Letter of Undertaking (LUT) so you don't pay IGST up front. Rules change, so confirm before your first large invoice.
  - Contracts should state the governing law, payment terms (a deposit before animation starts is standard), revision rounds, kill fees if a project is cancelled, and who owns the project files (clients often assume they get your After Effects project; that's a separate, priced deliverable).
- **Remote collaboration:** frame-accurate review tools (Frame.io, Resolve's collaboration, simple timecoded comment docs), shared project conventions and render-farm or cloud rendering when projects outgrow a laptop.

**Intuition anchors**
- Scope creep lives in undefined revision rounds. Define them.
- Your reel is a product. Edit it like one.

**Mastery checks**
- Write a scope with deliverables, rounds, timeline and change policy for a 60-second explainer.
- Explain the risks and rules of spec work.
- Outline a showreel and a case-study page.

**Analysis**
- Write a full proposal (treatment, styleframe plan, schedule, price structure, rights) for a 45-second launch film for EcoTrace.

**Taste**
- Professionalism in motion means predictable delivery. Clients return to designers who make the process calm.

## PART 8 — Genres in Depth: Explainers, Titles, Brand Films, Social & Idents [Core → Deep]

Parts 1–6 described one pipeline. In practice each genre bends it differently: what gets approved, where the craft goes, and what "done" looks like.

- **Explainers and product films** (the most commissioned genre, and the one EcoTrace needs):
  - **Structure:** hook (the viewer's problem, in the first 5 seconds) → problem (make it felt, briefly) → solution (the product in one sentence) → how it works (three steps at most) → proof (a number, a customer, a certification) → call to action. For 60–90 seconds, that is roughly one beat per 10–15 seconds.
  - **Visual strategy:** pick one metaphor system and stay inside it (a factory of data, a journey across a map, a desk seen from above) rather than a new visual idea per line of script. Real product UI should appear as **simplified, re-drawn screens**, not raw screen recordings: remove noise, enlarge the one control that matters, and animate the cursor with intent (ease, pause before click, 18 Part 4).
  - **Where the craft goes:** the script and the transitions between beats. The best explainers feel like one continuous shot because each scene's last shape becomes the next scene's first (match cuts, Part 3).
  - **Failure mode:** trying to explain every feature. The brief should name the *one* thing the viewer must remember.
- **Title sequences:**
  - **Jobs:** set tone, carry credits legibly, and often state the film's theme in abstract form before the story begins (Saul Bass's fractured arm in *The Man with the Golden Arm*; Kyle Cooper's hand-scratched *Se7en* type).
  - **Constraints:** credit order and sizes are usually contractual; each credit needs readable screen time; the sequence must work with the score. Main-on-end titles (credits over the end rather than the start) are increasingly common in streaming series, where the opening is short or skippable.
  - **Where the craft goes:** a single graphic idea developed with variation, and typography treated as a character.
- **Brand films and manifestos:** 30–120 seconds of mood and belief rather than information. The risk is emptiness: without a concrete idea, brand films become stock-footage montages with a voice-over. Ground them in one specific image or action that belongs only to this brand.
- **Logo animations and idents:** 2–5 seconds for a logo sting; channel **idents** (5–15 seconds) often come as a *system* of variations around one idea. The animation should reveal something true about the mark's construction or meaning (10 Part 4), have a clean final frame that holds long enough to register, and come in lengths and ratios for every placement (end cards, app splash, social avatar loops).
- **Social content (Reels, Shorts, stories):**
  - **The first second decides.** Open on motion and the most interesting frame, not a logo build. Assume sound-off (burned-in captions) but reward sound-on.
  - **Loops:** a seamless loop replays automatically and multiplies watch time. Design the last frame to match the first.
  - **Series thinking:** a repeatable format (a template, a recurring character, a signature transition) beats one-off pieces for building an audience, which matters if you run a faceless channel.
  - **Platform grammar changes quickly**; study what's native on each platform this month, and keep your master files flexible enough to re-cut.
- **Kinetic type and lyric videos:** timing to the syllable, legibility above all, and a typographic system (a few treatments reused) so the piece feels designed rather than randomly animated. For Hindi and Tamil lyrics, animate by word or line (18 Part 10) and check shaping in every frame.
- **Data stories and animated infographics:** one message per chart, staged transitions (18 Part 9), labels that stay readable, and a clear source line. The data-visualisation rules of 22 apply fully.
- **Event and stage visuals:** LED walls and projection for launches, concerts and conferences use extreme aspect ratios (often ultra-wide), very high resolutions, and loops that run for minutes. Check the actual pixel map of the screen and the frame rate of the playback system before designing.

**Intuition anchors**
- Every genre has one thing the viewer must leave with. Find it before you open the software.
- Explainers are scripts with pictures; titles are ideas with type; idents are marks with behaviour.
- On social, the hook and the loop are designed, not accidental.

**Mastery checks**
- Outline the explainer structure and time each beat for a 60-second piece.
- List the jobs and constraints of a title sequence.
- Explain what makes an ident system different from a single logo animation.
- Name four design rules specific to Reels and Shorts.

**Analysis**
- Take one genre per week for five weeks. Collect five examples, time their beats, and write one page on the genre's shared structure and the one example that breaks it successfully.

**Taste**
- Genre conventions are a starting grid, like a typographic grid. Learn them well enough to break one on purpose.

## PART 9 — Camera, Space & Cinematography for Motion Designers [Deep]

Motion designers work with a virtual camera, even in 2D. The language of cinematography tells you how.

- **Camera moves and what they mean:**
  - **Pan and tilt:** the camera rotates in place (left/right, up/down). It *surveys* or *reveals*.
  - **Dolly (push in / pull out):** the camera moves towards or away from the subject. A push-in builds intensity or intimacy; a pull-out reveals context or isolation.
  - **Truck and pedestal:** the camera moves sideways or up and down. With layered artwork, this creates **parallax**, the strongest cue for depth in 2.5D.
  - **Zoom:** the lens changes focal length without moving. It flattens and feels more mechanical than a dolly; the **dolly zoom** (moving in while zooming out, *Vertigo*'s effect) warps space for disorientation.
  - **Orbit and crane:** circling or rising moves, mostly in 3D, for product hero shots and establishing shots.
  - **Rack focus:** shifting focus from one depth to another, directing attention without moving the camera.
- **Lenses and perspective:** a wide lens (short focal length, e.g. 24 mm equivalent) exaggerates depth and speed; a long lens (85 mm and up) compresses space and makes backgrounds loom. After Effects, Blender and Cavalry cameras all expose focal length; choose it deliberately rather than leaving the default.
- **2.5D:** flat layers placed at different depths in 3D space, filmed with a moving camera. It's the classic way to animate illustrations and photographs (the "Ken Burns" move is its simplest form). Keep the parallax believable: distant layers move less; scale layers up as you push them back so they fill the frame.
- **Shot sizes and framing:** wide (context), medium (action), close-up (detail, emotion), extreme close-up (texture, drama). Varying shot size across a sequence controls pace just as cutting does. Framing rules from 02 (thirds, leading lines, negative space) apply per frame.
- **Screen direction and continuity:** keep movement directions consistent across cuts (if the parcel travels left to right, it keeps doing so), and respect the **180° rule** when two subjects face each other. Breaking direction reads as a reversal or a new idea, which you can use on purpose.
- **Motion blur and shutter angle:** real cameras blur movement during exposure. A **180° shutter** (exposure for half of each frame's duration) is the film look; wider angles smear more, narrower ones strobe. Adding motion blur to fast graphic moves makes them read as motion rather than as jumping shapes; leave it off for crisp UI demos.
- **Depth of field:** shallow focus isolates the subject and suggests scale (a very shallow focus makes large scenes look like miniatures). After Effects 26.3's depth of field, Blender's camera and Resolve's Fusion all simulate it. Use it to guide the eye, not everywhere.
- **Camera shake and handheld feel:** a small amount of procedural noise (wiggle expressions, Cavalry's Noise behaviour, Blender's noise modifier) makes impacts land and CG feel filmed. Too much becomes nausea (18 Part 5's vestibular concerns apply to video too).
- **The 3D-as-slides problem:** a camera flying past floating panels doesn't create meaning by itself (`TD-08#18 · @pragyamuthuraman · DSF4aTAEnI_`). Every camera move should reveal, relate or emphasise something.

**Intuition anchors**
- A camera move is a sentence. Know what it's saying before you add it.
- Parallax sells depth; focal length sells mood.
- Motion blur makes speed legible. Without it, fast moves strobe.

**Mastery checks**
- Name eight camera moves and the meaning each usually carries.
- Explain how focal length changes perspective and when you'd use a wide or long lens.
- Explain 2.5D parallax and the scaling rule for layers pushed back in depth.
- Explain shutter angle and when to add or remove motion blur.

**Analysis**
- Rebuild one 5-second moment from a film you admire as a 2.5D piece: cut a still into 4–6 depth layers (Krita or Photopea), place them in Cavalry or After Effects 3D space, and animate the camera move. Compare 24 mm and 85 mm versions.

**Taste**
- Great motion designers move the camera like cinematographers: rarely, deliberately, and always for a reason.

## PART 10 — Procedural, Generative & Data-Driven Motion [Deep → Frontier]

Keyframes describe motion one pose at a time. Procedural motion describes **rules**, and the software generates the poses. For a developer like you, this is the part of motion design where your coding brain is a direct advantage.

- **When procedural beats keyframes:**
  - Many similar elements (a grid of 400 dots, a field of particles, a crowd of icons).
  - Motion that must stay consistent across many versions (a template the client re-uses with new text and colours).
  - Motion driven by data or sound (a bar chart race from a CSV, visuals that react to music).
  - Organic randomness (flicker, drift, wobble) that would be tedious to keyframe.
  - Keyframes still win for **performance**: acting, precise timing to a VO, a hero move. Most real pieces combine both.
- **The toolkit:**
  - **After Effects expressions** (JavaScript): `wiggle()`, `loopOut()`, `valueAtTime()`, `linear()`/`ease()` for remapping one range to another, `index` for per-layer offsets, sliders and checkboxes on a controller null. After Effects also imports **JSON and CSV/TSV** files as footage that expressions can read, for data-driven animation.
  - **Cavalry** is procedural by design: **Duplicators** and distributions (grid, circle, path, random) create many shapes; **Behaviours** (Stagger, Noise, Oscillator, Falloff, Spring and more) animate them; **Spreadsheet** assets bring in CSV or Google Sheets data; **connections** wire any attribute to any other. It's free for individuals since April 2026, which makes it your default procedural tool (38 Part 7).
  - **Blender:** **drivers** (a property driven by an expression or another property), **Geometry Nodes** (a node system that generates and animates geometry procedurally), and modifiers with noise.
  - **TouchDesigner** (Derivative): node-based real-time visuals for installations, VJing, projection and audio-reactive work. Its free **Non-Commercial** licence (with an output resolution cap) is enough to learn on.
  - **Code:** **p5.js** and **Processing** (creative coding sketches), **three.js** with shaders (21 Part 6), and **Motion Canvas** or **Remotion** (write animated videos in TypeScript or React, render to MP4). Remotion lets you build data-driven videos with the React skills you already have, which makes it a strong fit for EcoTrace reports turned into video. (Remotion is free for individuals and very small companies; larger companies need a paid licence, so check its terms before client work. Motion Canvas is MIT-licensed.)
- **Generative design principles:**
  - **A system, a seed and constraints.** Define the rules (shapes, palette, spacing, motion behaviours), then vary a random seed to get a family of outputs. Constraints (a fixed palette, a grid, limited motion types) keep the family coherent.
  - **Curate, don't just generate.** The designer's job is choosing which outputs are good and tuning the rules until most are.
  - **Noise, not randomness.** Pure random values flicker; **Perlin or simplex noise** gives smooth, organic variation over time and space. Most "organic" procedural motion is noise sampled along time.
  - **Falloffs and fields:** a value that changes with distance from a point (a cursor, a null, a moving shape) makes groups of elements respond together, like wind through grass.
- **Audio-reactive motion:** convert audio to keyframes (After Effects's "Convert Audio to Keyframes", Cavalry's audio analysis, Blender's Graph Editor sound-baking tool or TouchDesigner's audio analysis) and drive scale, brightness or position with the amplitude of specific frequency bands. Smooth the signal, or everything jitters. The kick drum on scale and hi-hats on small details is a classic mapping.
- **Data-driven motion:** bar chart races, animated maps and live dashboards turned into video. Clean the data first (22), lock the scales so bars don't jump when the maximum changes, and label everything. Build the template once, then regenerate when the data changes.
- **Templates for others:** Essential Graphics templates (After Effects → Premiere), Cavalry's exposed controls and Resolve's Fusion macros let non-animators change text, colours and timing within safe limits. Good templates constrain choices so the result still looks designed.

**Intuition anchors**
- Keyframe the performance; proceduralise the crowd.
- Randomness flickers, noise breathes.
- A generative system is judged by its worst output, so tune the rules until the worst is acceptable.

**Mastery checks**
- Name five situations where procedural motion beats keyframing, and two where it doesn't.
- Explain Cavalry's Duplicators, Behaviours and Spreadsheets, and Blender's drivers and Geometry Nodes, in one sentence each.
- Explain the difference between random values and noise.
- Describe an audio-reactive mapping and how to keep it from jittering.

**Analysis**
- Build the same piece two ways: a 5-second grid of 100 circles that ripples outward from the centre, first by keyframing a few layers and offsetting them by hand, then with a Cavalry Duplicator and Stagger/Falloff behaviours. Compare time taken and how easy it is to change the number of circles to 400.
- Render a 10-second EcoTrace emissions bar chart race in Remotion from a CSV.

**Taste**
- Procedural work looks designed when the rules came from an idea, and looks like a plug-in demo when the rules came from the plug-in.

## PART 11 — Directing, Feedback & Team Pipelines [Deep]

Solo pieces teach craft. Commercial work teaches you to direct: to hold an idea steady while several people and several rounds of feedback push on it.

- **Director vs animator:** the animator asks "how do I make this move well?"; the director asks "what should this piece make people feel, and is every shot serving it?". On small jobs you're both. Switch hats deliberately: review your own work as the director before sending it.
- **Roles on a larger motion job:** creative director (idea and standards), art director / designer (styleframes and design boards), animators (2D, 3D, character), compositor, 3D generalist, sound designer and composer, editor, colourist, and a **producer** who owns schedule, budget and client communication. Knowing these roles helps you scope even when you're one person pretending to be six.
- **The style guide ("animation bible"):** when several people animate one piece or a series, write down the rules: palette, type treatments, stroke widths, the curve presets, standard durations for transitions, how characters move, camera rules, texture and grain settings, and render settings. It's a motion design system (18 Part 6) for a film.
- **Giving feedback:**
  - Watch the whole piece once without stopping, then note by **timecode**.
  - Separate **idea** notes (the message doesn't land), **timing** notes (hold longer at 00:12), **design** notes (the green is too saturated) and **technical** notes (flicker at 00:31).
  - Describe the problem and the goal, not only the fix ("I lose the product at 00:08; can it stay larger through the move?"), so the animator can find a better solution than yours.
  - Prioritise: must-fix, should-fix, optional.
- **Receiving client feedback:**
  - Translate vague notes ("make it pop", "more premium") into a question about a specific mechanism: contrast, pacing, colour, texture, type size (`TD-15#15 · @uiuxmanuel · Db82VC5juMd`).
  - Consolidate: ask for **one set of notes per round** from one decision-maker, not a stream of messages from five stakeholders.
  - Show changes side by side with the previous version and a changelog.
  - When a note would break an earlier approval (new script lines after the animatic was locked), say so and reference the scope (Part 7).
- **Review tooling:** frame-accurate review platforms (Frame.io, or DaVinci Resolve's collaboration and review features), a shared timecoded document for small jobs, burned-in timecode on review renders, and watermarks on drafts.
- **Pipeline hygiene for teams:** consistent folder structures and naming (Part 6), versioned files never overwritten, shared asset libraries (fonts, colour swatches, pre-comps), locked approved shots, and render-time budgets per shot. Pick a single source of truth for the edit and conform everything to it.
- **Schedules:** a realistic 60-second explainer with a new style, from brief to delivery, often runs several weeks: roughly a week for script and treatment, a week for styleframes and storyboard, a week for the animatic and design boards, two to three weeks for animation and sound, and a few days for revisions and delivery. Compress it by simplifying the style, not by skipping the animatic.
- **Burnout and pace:** motion is deadline-heavy. Protect the pre-production steps in the schedule, because they are what prevents all-night rendering and re-animation at the end.

**Intuition anchors**
- A note without a timecode is a mood.
- Direct the idea; delegate the keyframes.
- Protect the animatic stage; it's where the schedule is saved.

**Mastery checks**
- List the roles on a larger motion job and what each owns.
- Write a style-guide outline for a four-episode explainer series.
- Rewrite five vague client notes as specific, answerable questions.
- Draft a schedule for a 60-second explainer from brief to delivery.

**Analysis**
- Exchange a 20-second piece with a peer (or with Claude acting as a client). Give and receive a round of timecoded notes using the categories above, then write down which notes improved the piece and which were preferences.

**Taste**
- The best directors make everyone's work look like one person's. Consistency across hands is the mark of a directed piece.
