## Why motion exists in UI

Motion earns its place by doing one of five jobs: **explaining change** (continuity between states), **directing attention**, **giving feedback** (a press, a success), **expressing brand**, and **making waiting feel shorter**. Motion without one of these reasons is decoration that costs performance and accessibility.

## Transitions

`transition-property`, `-duration`, `-timing-function`, `-delay`, or the shorthand with several comma-separated transitions. **`transition: all` is an anti-pattern**: it animates things you didn't intend (a colour-scheme switch, a layout change) and costs performance. What can animate depends on each property's *animation type* (discrete properties flip instead of interpolating). `transitionrun`, `transitionstart`, `transitionend` and `transitioncancel` events let scripts follow along.

## Entry and exit from `display: none`

Elements that appear from `display: none` have no previous style to transition from. **`@starting-style`** provides it: the "before-open" values for an element's first style. On exit, **`transition-behavior: allow-discrete`** lets `display` (and `overlay` for top-layer elements) flip *after* the exit transition instead of immediately. Together they animate dialogs and popovers in and out with no JavaScript.

## Animating to `auto`

`height: auto` couldn't animate because `auto` isn't a number. Now **`interpolate-size: allow-keywords`** (set once on `:root`) and **`calc-size()`** **[Frontier]** let browsers interpolate to intrinsic sizes: accordions without measuring in JavaScript. Older techniques still matter as fallbacks: **grid-template-rows `0fr` → `1fr`**, max-height hacks (guessing a maximum), and FLIP.

## Easing

`linear`; `ease`; **`ease-in`** for exits (accelerate away); **`ease-out`** for entrances (the default choice for UI); `ease-in-out` for movement on screen; **`cubic-bezier()`** (read the curve; y values outside 0–1 overshoot); **`steps()`** for sprite animation and typewriters; and **`linear()`** with many stops for **spring and bounce approximations** in pure CSS. Easing is where motion gets its personality.

## Transforms

`translate`, `rotate`, `scale`, `skew`, `matrix`. The **individual transform properties** (`translate:`, `rotate:`, `scale:`) animate independently, so a hover scale doesn't fight a keyframed rotation. In the `transform` list, **order matters** (functions apply right to left: `rotate(45deg) translate(100px)` orbits; `translate(100px) rotate(45deg)` moves then spins). `transform-origin` sets the pivot (`transform-box: fill-box` for SVG). Transforms **don't affect layout**: siblings don't move.

Silent **flattening** triggers break 3D: `overflow` other than visible, `opacity < 1`, `filter` and `clip-path` on the preserve-3d element.

## FLIP

**First** (measure positions), **Last** (change the layout), **Invert** (apply a transform that puts each element back where it was), **Play** (animate the transform to none). The layout change happens instantly and only compositor-friendly transforms animate. It's the technique behind shared-element motion, list reordering and expanding cards; view transitions (Part 17) now automate much of it.

## Performance of motion

Animate **`transform` and `opacity`**: they're compositor-only, run off the main thread and stay smooth when JavaScript is busy. Avoid animating layout properties (`width`, `height`, `top`, `margin`) and paint-heavy ones (large `box-shadow` blurs, `filter` over big areas, `backdrop-filter`). Use **`will-change`** sparingly: it creates layers that cost memory (add before, remove after). Watch for layer explosions. Frame budgets: **16.7 ms at 60 Hz, 8.3 ms at 120 Hz**. Profile in the Performance panel and the Animations inspector.

## Accessibility of motion

**`prefers-reduced-motion`**: the recommended pattern is to **reduce, not remove**: replace large movement (slides, zooms, parallax) with fades, keep feedback, and gate big motion behind `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference)`. Motion can trigger **vestibular disorders** (dizziness, nausea). WCAG **2.3.3** (motion from interactions can be disabled), **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide** (anything moving automatically for more than 5 s needs a control), and **2.3.1 Three Flashes**. Parallax and zoom are the most common triggers.

## Motion design principles

### The 12 principles, for interfaces

Disney's principles (Thomas & Johnston) translate: **squash and stretch** (subtle, for tactile presses), **anticipation** (a slight wind-up), **staging** (one thing at a time), **follow-through and overlapping action** (parts settle at different times), **slow in and slow out** (easing), **arcs** (natural paths curve), **secondary action**, **timing**, **exaggeration** (sparingly) and **appeal**.

### Durations, choreography and systems

**Durations**: micro-interactions 100–200 ms, small transitions 200–300 ms, larger panels 300–500 ms, full-screen transitions up to ~500 ms; **exits faster than entrances**; distance and size scale duration; nothing the user waits for runs over ~400 ms without purpose. **Choreography**: stagger (with `sibling-index()` **[Frontier]** or custom properties), a hierarchy (the primary element moves first), shared direction (things enter from where they come from), consistent physics. **Motion as a system**: duration and easing tokens (this site uses 150/250/400 ms with standard, out, in and spring easings), named patterns (enter, exit, expand, emphasise), and references like Material motion, Apple's HIG, IBM Carbon and Fluent.

## Springs and physics

Springs feel natural because they're **velocity-aware** and **interruptible**: a spring that's re-targeted mid-flight keeps its momentum instead of restarting. Parameters: **stiffness**, **damping**, **mass** (or, designer-friendly, response and bounce). CSS approximates a spring with **`linear()`** (the stops below were generated at build time by this site's motion library); reach for libraries (Motion, React Spring, GSAP) for interruption, gestures and velocity hand-off.
