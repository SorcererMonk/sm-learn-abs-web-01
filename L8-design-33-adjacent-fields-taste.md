> This is the **learning** file for taste: what it is, how it is actually built, and where it comes from outside design itself. It covers the mechanics of taste development (exposure, deliberate comparison, articulation, making), how to build a reference library and a critical vocabulary, and twelve adjacent fields — architecture and interiors, fashion and textiles, film and cinematography, photography, music, fine art, craft (with a strong Indian thread), literature and poetry, game design, product and industrial design, science and engineering, and law — each with what a designer can take from it and one exercise to take it. It covers Indian design and art culture on verified fact, the Pinterest/Dribbble sameness problem and algorithmic taste, taste versus trend, and how taste shows up as a product decision — what you choose to leave out — worked through EcoTrace and Meridian UI. It closes with the evidence on how users actually perceive "quality" and a lifelong taste practice. It deliberately stops where neighbours take over: design philosophy and the craft/judgement distinction are `L8-design-01-mindset-philosophy.md`; the mechanics of visual principles and their evidence base are `L8-design-02-theory-principles.md`; the full history of movements, including Indian design history, is `L8-design-08-history-movements.md`; ethics of borrowing and cultural appropriation are `L8-design-31-ethics-philosophy.md`; AI-assisted generation is `L8-design-30-generative-ai-assisted.md`. The training counterpart is `T8-design-33-adjacent-fields-taste.md`.
>
> **Context stamp (September 2026):** production has never been cheaper — Figma's 2026 tools (Weave, Figma Agent, Motion, Draw) and every AI model can generate a competent screen in seconds. That makes **evaluation the scarce skill**, not generation (`TD-13#13`). The most-named "expensive" look in current SaaS interfaces is **layering** — stacked translucent planes and glows rather than drop shadows (`TD-13#30`) — which is a trend, not a principle, and will date. The recurring anxiety in design culture this year is **sameness**: designers who pull reference from the same Pinterest boards and Dribbble feeds produce work that "rhymes" (`TD-13#15`), because shared inputs are arithmetic, not a failure of imagination. Geographical Indication (GI) tags on Indian crafts — Channapatna toys (Karnataka), Pattachitra (Odisha), Madhubani/Mithila painting (Bihar), and Bhoodan Pochampally Ikat (Telangana) among them — are live legal certifications administered under India's Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999, not just historical references; they matter to a law student building taste from craft. The aesthetic–usability effect remains the best-evidenced link between beauty and perceived quality, but the 1995 study that named it (Kurosu & Kashimura) has been retested and qualified many times since, and this file gives you the nuance, not just the headline.

**The taste-development learning loop:**

2. **Compare, don't just admire.** Put two things that do the same job side by side and name what's different.
3. **Articulate in writing, every time.** A feeling you can't explain isn't yet a principle you can use (Part 1).
4. **Borrow across fields on purpose.** Design is not the only discipline that has solved composition, restraint, rhythm or trust (Parts 3–7).
5. **Make, then compare your own work to the reference.** Consuming without making caps you at recognising good work, not producing it.
6. **Revisit your own older work every few months.** If it doesn't embarrass you slightly, you haven't grown.

## PART 0 — Foundations: What Taste Is [Core]

- **Taste is a trained capacity to judge, not an innate gift.** It is the ability to tell that one solution is better than another for a given audience and purpose, and to say why. Believing taste is a gift makes it fixed; believing it is a habit makes it a schedule you can run (`TD-13#1`, "creativity is a habit, not a gift").
- **Taste is not the same as skill, and the space between them has a name.** Ira Glass's widely circulated 2010 remarks on creative work (from an interview for Current TV, "Ira Glass on Storytelling: Part 3") describe a phase that "most everybody I know who does interesting creative work" goes through: good enough taste to know your own work "wasn't as good as they wanted it to be," while the ability to close that distance hasn't caught up yet. Glass's own conclusion is not to wait it out but to keep producing: "the most important thing you can do is do a lot of work." This is usually shortened to **"the gap"** — the period where your taste has outrun your hands.
  - **The gap is not a flaw to fix before you start.** It is evidence the taste is real. Beginners with no gap either have no taste yet or aren't looking closely at their own work.
  - **The only way through is volume**, not more consumption. Watching more reference footage doesn't close the gap; making more things, and comparing them plainly to the reference, does.
- **Taste is not the same as opinion.** An opinion ("I don't like this") is a reaction. Taste is a reaction plus a reason plus a track record of reasons that hold up against outcomes. Someone with taste can tell you *why* something doesn't work and predict what would fix it.
- **Taste is not universal, and treating it as if it were is itself a taste failure.** Dieter Rams's ten principles are an excellent description of one tradition — reductive, functionalist, electronics-era industrial design — and applying them as a universal standard quietly delegitimises ornament, craft, joy and decoration, which are functions too (`TD-13#18`). A designer with real taste can hold a rule and its exception at once.
- **Taste can make work worse.** Applying stylistic sophistication where the job needed clarity is a documented failure mode: plain, "ugly" design frequently outperforms polished design because it reads as direct rather than styled, and because when the visuals recede, the copy does the persuading (`TD-13#3`, "why ugly wins"). Taste maximalism — sophistication as a reflex — is itself bad taste.
- **The Art / Craft / Design model** is a useful frame for where a given decision sits. Picture three overlapping circles — **Art**, **Craft**, **Design** — with the overlaps labelled: **intuitive** (art ∩ craft), **planned** (art ∩ design), **functional** (craft ∩ design), and **creative** at the centre where all three meet (`TD-13#21`). Placing your own current project on this diagram is diagnostic: a component library exercise sits mostly in craft ∩ design (functional); a personal side project may sit in art ∩ craft (intuitive). Knowing which region you're in tells you which standard to hold yourself to.

**Intuition anchors**
- Taste is judgement with a track record, not a feeling with no history.
- The gap between taste and skill is proof the taste is working, not proof you're a fraud.
- A rule that can't name its own exceptions isn't taste, it's dogma.

**Mastery checks**
- Define taste in a way that distinguishes it from skill and from opinion.
- State Ira Glass's "gap" argument and his prescribed way through it, in your own words.
- Explain why "ugly wins" is a taste argument, not an anti-taste argument.
- Place a current project of yours on the Art/Craft/Design Venn diagram and justify the placement.

**Analysis**
- Pick three pieces of your own work from the last year. For each, write one paragraph naming what you were trying to achieve and where it fell short. If none fall short, you're not looking closely enough — go back and look again.

**Taste**
- The people whose taste you respect most are usually the ones who can tell you precisely what's wrong with their own last piece of work, not just what's wrong with everyone else's.

## PART 1 — How Taste Develops: Exposure, Comparison, Articulation, Making [Core]

- **Taste is built on four repeatable mechanisms, not one.** Skipping any one of them caps how far the others can take you.
  1. **Exposure.** Seeing a large, deliberately chosen volume of work — not a feed's volume, a curated one. "Taste is the residue of hours spent in front of great work" (`TD-13#14`). Residue is the right word: you cannot study taste directly, only accumulate the exposure that produces it.
  2. **Deliberate comparison.** Two things that solve the same problem, placed side by side, force you to notice what a single piece never reveals. A large-sample survey approach — studying thousands of examples of one interface type to see what recurs — teaches conventions; studying five exceptional examples in depth teaches when to break them. Both matter: breadth for convention, depth for departure (`TD-13#20`).
  3. **Articulation.** Explaining, in writing, why something feels off (or right) is "the single best taste exercise" in wide circulation, because a vague reaction stays useless until it becomes a stated principle you can reapply (`TD-13#7`). "Consuming is not learning" — you must annotate, not just look (`TD-13#11`).
  4. **Making.** Comparing your own output to the reference, then closing the distance. Consumption alone caps you at *recognising* quality; only making, repeatedly, builds the ability to *produce* it.
- **A schedulable habit list**, drawn from named, specific practice (not aspiration):
  - Explain in writing why something feels off, every time you notice it (`TD-13#7`, `TD-13#11` habit 6).
  - Watch films and interfaces **muted, on a big screen** — removing audio forces your eyes to carry the analysis (`TD-13#11` habit 3).
  - Analyse the software you already use daily, **on paper, with a pen** — slower analysis is more analytical than scrolling past it (`TD-13#17`).
  - Stay bored on purpose: 30 minutes a day with no input and no agenda, or "someone else's content or algorithm will keep shaping your beliefs forever" (`TD-13#11` habit 7).
  - Study **poor and ugly** products deliberately, not only admired ones — the gap between them is where the principle lives (`TD-13#11` habit 4).
  - Question invisible defaults: ask why the ordinary, unremarkable thing in front of you looks the way it does (`TD-13#11` habit 5).
  - **Create more than you consume** — repeated across this catalogue by more than one source, which is itself a signal of how load-bearing it is (`TD-13#7`, `TD-13#17`).
- **Analog input has a specific advantage over digital browsing.** Printed magazines, packaging and book covers give your senses more data points than a screen — weight, texture, print quality, physical scale — and teach you how design behaves in a context a feed can't show (`TD-13#11` habit 1). Discussing what you noticed out loud with another person surfaces insights that stay latent when you keep them to yourself (`TD-13#11` habit 2).
- **Taste transfers across senses and disciplines before it transfers within your own field.** Steve Jobs's calligraphy course, later cited as shaping the typographic sensibility behind the Mac, is the canonical example of consuming "out of algorithm" — something with zero apparent relevance to your work that changes how you see it (`TD-13#11` habit 8). Parts 3–7 below apply this systematically across twelve fields.
- **Two failure modes to name and avoid:**
  - **Collecting instead of absorbing.** Assembling a moodboard feels like work and barely engages you with any individual image; watching one difficult film through, or following one person's output deeply over years, changes how you see far more (`TD-13#25`). This directly contradicts the "build a moodboard" advice found elsewhere in design culture — both views exist and are worth weighing, but collecting without absorbing is the weaker of the two.
  - **Learning mistaken for mastering.** Watching tutorials teaches you where the tool's buttons are; only repeated practice converts that into fluency. Confusing the two is why watching more tutorials stops working (`TD-13#29`, paraphrased: software learning happens on YouTube, software mastery happens through regular practice).

**Intuition anchors**
- Exposure without articulation produces a vague sense of quality that you can recognise but can't reproduce.
- The habit that yields the most per minute spent is writing down why something feels off.
- Watching a tutorial and being able to do the thing are different skills entirely.

**Mastery checks**
- Name the four mechanisms of taste development and explain what fails if you skip one.
- Explain the difference between collecting and absorbing, with an example of each from your own recent behaviour.
- State why analog input (print, physical objects) has an advantage a feed doesn't.

**Analysis**
- For one week, keep a running note of every time something felt "off" in an interface, film, product or space you encountered. Write one sentence of *why* for each. At the end of the week, look for repeats — a repeated "why" is an emerging principle of your own.

**Taste**
- If your explanation of why something is good could apply to almost anything, it isn't yet a real judgement. Specificity is the test.

## PART 2 — Building a Reference Library & Critical Vocabulary [Core]

- **Archive, not gallery, is the operative distinction.** A gallery (a feed, a trend board) shows you what's currently popular. An archive shows you what was made, indexed and credited, across decades — searchable, credited, documented (`TD-13#4`, `TD-13#14`). If everyone browses the same galleries, everyone converges on the same references; archives are where divergence comes from (`TD-13#15`).
  - **Named, credited archives worth knowing:** Archives.design (a digital archive of graphic design history), Typographic Jazz (an online type exhibition), the New York Public Library's digital collections, and The People's Graphic Design Archive — all free, all citable by name and date, unlike a screenshot with no provenance (`TD-13#4`, `TD-13#14`, `TD-13#15`).
  - **Pentagram.com** is a standout, under-used resource for brand and identity work specifically: decades of major identity projects, each with a written case study explaining the reasoning behind the decisions, from some of the most respected partners in the discipline — a free archive of *rationale*, not just outcomes (`TD-13#24`).
- **A critical vocabulary is what turns a reaction into a transferable principle.** Studio shorthand exists for a reason — it lets a team discuss a judgement quickly once everyone shares the terms (`TD-13#12`):
  - **"Kill your darlings"** — delete the parts you love if they don't serve the work. The only entry in the studio-idiom list that's a discipline rather than decoration, and the hardest to actually do, because the element you're most attached to is usually the one you spent longest on.
  - **"That's a feature, not a bug"** — what looks like a mistake is intentional; use it as it's meant, not as a dodge.
  - **"Eye for detail"** — noticing, not just looking; the difference between scanning and zooming in.
  - **Watch for the vocabulary being used as performance rather than judgement.** "Iterations, refinements" is a phrase designers use to signal seriousness, often in place of actually answering *who is this for* — a satirised but real failure mode (`TD-13#2`). The test: can you say precisely what changed and why, or only that you "iterated"?
- **Precision matters more than vocabulary volume.** "Wabi-sabi" is routinely misused as permission to be sloppy. The correct sequencing is **craft first, then subtle imperfection on purpose at the very end** (`TD-13#19`). Imperfection that precedes craft isn't a style, it's an absence of craft. This ordering rule generalises to every "deliberately rough" aesthetic you'll be asked to imitate: brutalist type, grunge textures, hand-drawn UI accents. Ask, in each case, whether the roughness sits on top of resolved work or instead of it.
- **Curated sources reduce the labour but reintroduce the sameness risk.** Subscribing to a curated design newsletter buys back the unpaid hours of hunting for inspiration yourself, but a shared newsletter is still a shared feed — if everyone reads the same three, the rhyming problem returns with a nicer interface (`TD-13#22`). Mix subscriptions with archives you found yourself.
- **Named, free curricula worth building a library from**, all verified in this catalogue as free at time of writing:
  - Ten channels, ten videos and five playlists covering fundamentals → research → systems → portfolio, with **Nielsen Norman Group** as the research-grounded anchor and IDEO's "Human-Centered Design" for methodology (`TD-13#9`).
  - Three free videos covering colour theory, visual hierarchy and principles of design — roughly a foundation year's core content (`TD-13#23`).
  - Interface-taste specifically: *Invisible Details* (Dmytro), *Devouring Details* (Rauno, in three units: Principles, Prototypes, Resources) and *AI for Designers and Engineers* (Emil Kowalski) — the last one's framing, "build great interfaces with AI, without lowering your bar," is the whole AI-and-taste tension in one line (`TD-13#6`).

**Intuition anchors**
- An archive with no credit line teaches you to like things; a credited archive teaches you references you can name.
- A vocabulary word is only useful if you can say what it costs you to apply it — "kill your darlings" costs something; "iterations, refinements" costs nothing.
- If your explanation of a rough aesthetic can't distinguish it from a lack of effort, you haven't earned the roughness yet.

**Mastery checks**
- Name three credited archives and explain why they differ from a moodboard or a feed.
- Explain the wabi-sabi ordering rule and give a UI example of it done correctly and one done as an excuse for sloppiness.
- Give an example of studio vocabulary used as genuine judgement and one used as performance.

**Analysis**
- Build a personal reference library with at least three archive sources (not feeds) and one newsletter. For one project you're currently working on, pull two references from the archive and write, for each, exactly what you're taking and what you're deliberately not taking.

**Taste**
- The size of someone's reference library matters less than whether they can tell you, unprompted, who made each piece in it and why they kept it.

## PART 3 — Cross-Pollination I: Architecture & Interiors, Fashion & Textiles [Core]

- **Architecture and interiors.**
  - **What to take:** architects design for bodies moving through space over time, which is the closest analogue to a user moving through a flow. **Procession** (the sequence of spaces someone passes through, and what's revealed at each turn) maps directly onto onboarding and multi-step flows: what do you reveal first, what stays hidden until it's needed. **Materiality and tactility** — the reason a considered interior uses three finishes, not eight — maps onto restraint in a colour and elevation system. **Threshold design** (doorways, transitions between rooms) maps onto page and modal transitions: architects treat the moment of passing from one space to another as a deliberate design decision, not a byproduct.
  - **Exercise:** walk through one building you know well (a library, a station, your own home) and diagram it as a user flow: entry, decision points, dead ends, the one room that surprises you. Redraw one screen flow in your product using the same diagram vocabulary — entry, decision points, dead ends, the one screen that should surprise the user.
- **Fashion and textiles.**
  - **What to take:** a **collection** is a system, not a set of unrelated pieces — a small number of fabrics, a controlled palette, and repeated silhouettes recombined, which is the same discipline as a design token set and component library. **Seasonality and cadence** teach you to think about a product's visual identity as something that can refresh on a schedule without breaking its underlying grammar. **Pattern and weave** (Indian textile traditions especially — Ikat's resist-dyeing before weaving, Banarasi brocade's structural gold-thread patterning, block printing's repeat units) are literal, physical exercises in rhythm, repetition and controlled variation, the same vocabulary Part 2 of `L8-design-02` teaches for visual composition.
  - **Exercise:** pick one clothing collection (a designer lookbook, or a single well-made Indian textile tradition such as Ikat or block print) and list its constraints: how many fabrics, how many colours, what repeats and what varies. Apply the same constraint budget — a fixed number of fonts, colours and component variants — to your next screen.

**Intuition anchors**
- A building's procession and a product's onboarding are solving the same problem: what do you reveal, in what order, and why.
- A collection's discipline (few fabrics, controlled palette, recombined silhouettes) is a design-token system wearing different clothes, literally.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain "threshold design" in architecture and name one interface equivalent.
- Explain why a fashion collection and a component library are structurally the same kind of system.

**Analysis**
- Take one flow in your own product and annotate it using architectural procession language (entry, threshold, reveal, dead end). Note where the flow reveals information too early or too late.

**Taste**
- Restraint in materials (three finishes, not eight; three fonts, not eight) reads as confidence in every discipline that has ever tried it.

## PART 4 — Cross-Pollination II: Film & Cinematography, Photography, Music [Core]

- **Film and cinematography.**
  - **What to take:** **framing and staging** — what's in focus, what's cropped out, where the eye is directed first — is the same discipline as visual hierarchy applied to a moving image instead of a static screen. **Pacing** (the rhythm of cuts, held shots and silence) maps onto the pacing of an onboarding flow or a scroll-triggered narrative: a film that cuts every half-second exhausts you the same way a screen that animates everything does. **Colour grading** — a single, deliberate colour treatment applied across an entire film — is the same discipline as a product's colour system applied consistently across every screen and state.
  - **Exercise:** watch one scene from a film you admire with the sound off (`TD-13#11` habit 3). Storyboard it in six frames, noting only what's in frame and what's cropped. Apply the same six-frame discipline to a screen flow you're designing: what's in frame (visible, prioritised) at each step, and what's deliberately cropped out.
- **Photography.**
  - **What to take:** **composition rules** (rule of thirds, leading lines, negative space) are the same vocabulary as layout composition, but photography forces you to compose in a single frame with no layers to hide behind — a useful discipline for a designer who's used to fixing composition with a z-index. **Light** as the primary tool for directing attention (not colour, not size) is an underused lesson in interface design, where elevation and shadow are the closest equivalent.
  - **Exercise:** take ten photographs of the same static object using only changes in light and angle, no props. Apply the same constraint to a UI card: redesign it ten times changing only elevation, contrast and spacing, with no new colours or icons.
- **Music.**
  - **What to take:** **rhythm, tempo and dynamics** (loud/soft, dense/sparse) map onto pacing and density in an interface — a screen that's loud everywhere has no dynamics, the same failure as music with no quiet passages. **Motif and variation** — a theme introduced, then recombined throughout a piece — is the literal structure of a design system: a small set of primitives (motifs) recombined into every screen (variations). **Silence as a compositional choice**, not an absence, is the direct analogue of white space.
  - **Exercise:** listen to one piece of music you know well and mark, on a timeline, every moment of relative silence or sparseness. Compare that timeline to a screen in your product: mark every area of visual "silence" (white space, unstyled rest). If your screen's timeline has none, it's louder than the music you just analysed.

**Intuition anchors**
- A film that cuts constantly and a screen that animates constantly fail for the identical reason: no dynamics, so nothing reads as significant.
- Light, in photography, does the job that elevation and shadow do in an interface — it's the tool for saying "look here" without changing colour or size.
- A design system is a musical motif: a small set of primitives, recombined with variation, held together by a recognisable theme.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain how film pacing maps onto onboarding-flow pacing, with a concrete failure example from either.
- Explain why "silence" and "white space" are the same compositional idea under two names.

**Analysis**
- Storyboard one flow in your product in six frames, using only "what's in frame" and "what's cropped." Identify one screen where too much is in frame at once.

**Taste**
- The discipline photographers have that most digital designers lack is composing without layers to fix mistakes in later — try it once and your layered work gets better too.

## PART 5 — Cross-Pollination III: Fine Art & Craft (with a Strong Indian Thread) [Core]

- **Fine art.**
  - **What to take:** art gives you permission to ask "what is this actually doing" before "does this look good," and it trains you to sit with ambiguity — a resolved-looking interface can still be answering the wrong question, and fine art is the discipline most practiced at noticing that gap. The **Art/Craft/Design Venn diagram** (Part 0) is worth returning to here: fine art sits mostly in the art∩craft (intuitive) region, and borrowing from it means borrowing the intuitive move, not the finished look.
  - **Exercise:** spend thirty minutes with one gallery or archive artwork (not a screenshot — an archive per Part 2) and write down what question the piece seems to be asking, before describing what it looks like. Do the same exercise on your own current screen: what question is it actually answering, separate from how it looks.
- **Craft, with Indian traditions as the primary material.** Craft sits in the craft∩design overlap of the Venn diagram — functional, made by hand, repeated at scale with controlled variation — and India's craft traditions are a living, not historical, resource for this.
  - **Verified, GI-tagged Indian crafts worth studying directly, not through a moodboard:** **Madhubani (Mithila) painting** from Bihar — bold outlines, no empty space, natural pigments, a composition discipline that fills the frame deliberately rather than by accident; **Pattachitra** from Odisha — narrative scroll painting with a fixed border-and-frame grammar, useful for studying how a tradition constrains composition without limiting content; **Channapatna toys** from Karnataka — lacquered wood turning, a masterclass in form reduced to what a lathe and a child's hand both require; **Bhoodan Pochampally Ikat** from Telangana — resist-dyeing the yarn before weaving so the pattern is structural, not printed on top, the textile equivalent of building a pattern into a design token rather than styling it after the fact; **Kalamkari** from Andhra Pradesh — hand-painted or block-printed narrative cloth with a restrained, repeatable palette drawn from natural dyes. These are administered as Geographical Indications under India's GI Act, 1999 — a legal, not just cultural, recognition, which matters directly to a law student's reading of them.
  - **What to take from craft specifically:** every one of these traditions solves "how do I produce a consistent, high-quality result by hand, at volume, with a small and disciplined set of moves" — which is the exact problem a design system solves in software. A block-print repeat unit is a component; the printer's fixed palette is a token set; the resist-dye process that makes the pattern structural rather than applied is the difference between a design decision made in the system and one patched on top of it.
  - **On borrowing respectfully:** cite the tradition and the region by name (as above), don't flatten a specific named craft into generic "ethnic" or "tribal" styling, and where a project uses a motif commercially, credit and, where appropriate, source from the artisans and GI-registered producers rather than a stock-pattern library. This is developed further in `L8-design-31-ethics-philosophy.md`.
  - **Wabi-sabi, revisited:** the craft-first-then-imperfection ordering rule from Part 2 (`TD-13#19`) is visible directly in these traditions — a Channapatna toy's lacquer finish is resolved and precise; a Kalamkari cloth's hand-painted line has controlled, not accidental, variation. The imperfection you can see in hand craft is disciplined, not careless.

**Intuition anchors**
- A block-print repeat unit is a component. A dyer's fixed palette is a token set. Craft solved design systems before software needed the phrase.
- Naming the region and tradition (Bihar, Odisha, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh) is part of the citation, not decoration.

**Mastery checks**
- Name three GI-tagged Indian crafts, their regions, and the specific design lesson each teaches about systems or composition.
- Explain the difference between citing a named craft tradition and "ethnic" styling with no named source.

**Analysis**
- Choose one of the five crafts above and trace, from an archive or documented source (not a moodboard), how its constraint set (palette, tools, repeat unit) produces its recognisable result. Write down the equivalent constraint set you could impose on a component you're designing.

**Taste**
- The most durable design systems, including centuries-old textile traditions, are the ones where the constraint came first and the beauty is what the constraint produced — not the other way round.

## PART 6 — Cross-Pollination IV: Literature & Poetry, Game Design, Product & Industrial Design [Core]

- **Literature and poetry.**
  - **What to take:** **economy of language** — poetry's discipline of saying the most with the fewest words — is the direct model for microcopy and empty states (`L8-design-17-microcopy-ux-writing.md`). **Structure and pacing in prose** (chapter breaks, cliffhangers, the placement of information the reader needs versus wants) maps onto progressive disclosure: what do you reveal now, what earns a "chapter break" (a new screen), what's withheld for tension or clarity. **Voice** — a consistent narrator's perspective across a whole work — is what a brand voice guideline is trying to formalise.
  - **Exercise:** rewrite one paragraph of your product's copy (an empty state, an error, an onboarding line) as a haiku or a five-line poem, forcing yourself to keep only the essential words. Bring back only the words that survive the compression.
- **Game design.**
  - **What to take:** games are the discipline most explicitly built around **feedback loops, onboarding without instructions, and rewarding correct behaviour immediately** — a tutorial level teaches a mechanic by making it impossible to fail at first, then raising the stakes, which is a direct model for progressive onboarding. **Difficulty curves** map onto feature complexity curves: introduce one variable at a time. Games are also the most advanced discipline at **dark patterns** (loot boxes, artificial scarcity, guilt-based re-engagement prompts) — study them to recognise the same patterns when a growth team proposes them for your product (`L8-design-27-gamification-behavioural.md` covers this in full).
  - **Exercise:** play the first five minutes of a game you've never played, with the explicit goal of noting every moment it teaches you a mechanic without an instruction screen. List the equivalent five-minute-first-use moments in your own product and check whether any of them still require an instruction screen.
- **Product and industrial design.**
  - **What to take:** industrial design solves for **manufacturing constraint, ergonomics and the physical lifecycle of an object** (how it's held, how it wears, how it's repaired or discarded) — a discipline digital design mostly gets to skip, which is exactly why it's worth studying: it forces you to think about your product's entire lifecycle (onboarding, daily use, edge cases, offboarding, deletion) rather than just the screen in front of you. **Dieter Rams's ten principles**, read with the Part 0 caveat that they describe one tradition, are still the sharpest existing checklist for "does this need to exist in this form" (`L8-design-23-industrial-hardware.md` covers the discipline in full).
  - **Exercise:** pick one physical object you use daily and evaluate it against as many of Rams's ten principles as you can recall, noting where it fails. Apply the same checklist to one screen in your product, including "as little design as possible" — what could you remove and lose nothing.

**Intuition anchors**
- A game's tutorial level and a product's onboarding are solving the identical problem: teach one mechanic by making it briefly impossible to fail.
- Industrial design's forced attention to an object's whole lifecycle is a corrective for how easily digital design forgets what happens after "ship."

**Mastery checks**
- Explain progressive disclosure using a literary structure analogy (chapter breaks, withheld information).
- Name two things game design does exceptionally well and one thing it does exploitatively, and explain the difference.

**Analysis**
- Map your product's first five minutes of use against a difficulty curve: what's the first "mechanic" a new user must learn, and is it taught by doing or by reading instructions?

**Taste**
- The best onboarding in software was solved by game designers first; most product teams are still relearning it from scratch.

## PART 7 — Cross-Pollination V: Science & Engineering, and Law — Legal Drafting as Information Design [Core → Deep]

- **Science and engineering.**
  - **What to take:** the scientific method's discipline of **stating a hypothesis before you look at the data** is the direct ancestor of stating a design hypothesis before a usability test, rather than rationalising results afterwards (`L8-design-24-design-research.md`). **Engineering's obsession with failure modes** (what breaks, under what load, and what happens when it does) maps onto edge-case and error-state design — a discipline of asking "what's the empty state, the loading state, the offline state, the state where the API times out" before it happens in production, not after a user reports it. **Elegance-as-simplicity** in engineering (the fewest moving parts that satisfy the constraint) is the same taste judgement as visual restraint, applied to a system instead of a screen.
  - **Exercise:** for one feature you're building, write a one-line hypothesis ("users will complete X faster if Y") before you design anything, then design to test that hypothesis specifically, not just to look good.
- **Law — legal drafting as information design.** This is Mohit's own field, and it is a genuine, underused source of design taste, not just a professional credential to mention in a portfolio.
  - **Plain-language law** is a decades-old, cross-jurisdiction movement to write legal and regulatory text so a lay reader can understand it — the UK's Plain English Campaign (founded 1979) and the US federal Plain Writing Act (2010, requiring plain language in public-facing federal documents) are the two best-known institutional pushes. The underlying discipline — active voice, one idea per sentence, defined terms used consistently, no needless Latin — is UX writing's older sibling, and a law student already has formal training in it that most UX writers pick up informally.
  - **Contract design and visual contracts** are a live, named research field. Helena Haapio and Stefania Passera's work on **"contract visualisation"** (published across legal-design venues from the early 2010s onward) treats a contract as a user-facing document that should be designed for comprehension — using layout, icons, timelines, flowcharts and comparison tables to communicate obligations, not just prose clauses. **Cartoon Contracts** (a real, practising initiative by South African attorney Robert de Rooy) replaces standard prose contracts with comic-strip panels for low-literacy contexts, and reports meaningfully improved comprehension and compliance among signatories — a working proof that "the contract" and "the design of the contract" are not separable.
  - **What a designer can take from legal drafting specifically:** a well-drafted clause defines its terms once and uses them consistently thereafter — exactly the discipline a design system's naming convention requires and most component libraries lack. A contract's structure (definitions, then obligations, then remedies, in a fixed order) is information architecture with centuries of refinement behind it. A lawyer's habit of asking "what does this word mean if a court has to interpret it adversarially" is the same rigour a designer should apply to a button label a confused, adversarial (or just tired) user will interpret literally.
  - **What a lawyer-designer can take back to law:** every principle in this file — hierarchy, restraint, progressive disclosure, testing for comprehension rather than assuming it — applies directly to contract and policy design, an area Indian legal and regulatory practice is only beginning to professionalise. Building a visual, plain-language version of one contract type is a portfolio piece with almost no competition.
  - **Exercise:** take one clause from a real or template contract (a terms-of-service clause, a lease clause, an NDA clause) and redesign it as a one-page visual document — a flowchart, an obligations table, or an annotated diagram — that a non-lawyer could correctly explain back to you after reading it once.

**Intuition anchors**
- A failure-mode analysis in engineering and an edge-case list in design are the same document with a different name.
- A contract that only a lawyer can parse has failed at information design even if it's legally airtight.

**Mastery checks**
- State the plain-language law movement's core discipline and name one institutional example.
- Explain what "contract visualisation" is and name one real initiative that practises it.
- Explain why stating a hypothesis before designing matters, with an example.

**Analysis**
- Redesign one real or template legal clause as a visual, plain-language document per the exercise above. Test it by having someone with no legal training explain it back to you; note exactly where they get it wrong.

**Taste**
- The discipline of writing a clause that cannot be misread by a hostile reader is one of the purest design skills there is — most UX writers have never had to meet that bar, and most lawyers have never been told it's a design skill at all.

## PART 8 — Indian Design & Art Culture: A Working Resource, Not a Reference Aesthetic [Core]

- **The Eames Report (1958) and the founding of NID.** Charles and Ray Eames were invited by the Government of India to study the state of design and recommend a path forward; their resulting *India Report* (April 1958) argued for a design education institution grounded in both Indian craft traditions and modern industrial method. This led directly to the founding of the **National Institute of Design (NID), Ahmedabad, in 1961** — one of the earliest government-backed design schools in the world outside Europe and the US, predating equivalent institutions in many other countries. The report itself is a worthwhile primary source: it treats Indian craft not as a heritage to preserve behind glass but as a live design resource to build on, which is the same argument Part 5 makes about GI-tagged crafts.
- **India as a design-culture case study in trust, not just aesthetics.** A well-documented piece of design-cultural analysis contrasts a typical Indian e-commerce product page — dense with trust signals (secure transactions, zero-hassle returns, order tracking, customer support, free delivery, all stacked above the product) — with the cleaner pages a Western designer might instinctively want to impose (`TD-13#28`). The argument: those badges are doing **necessary functional work** in a market where brand reputation, consumer-protection enforcement and delivery reliability can't be assumed the way they might be assumed elsewhere. Minimalism presupposes a level of trust the brand may not yet have earned. This is not an excuse for clutter — it's a reminder that "clean" is a design decision available to brands whose customers already trust them, and a large share of Indian D2C and SaaS product design correctly does not have that luxury yet.
- **Typography in an Indian context.** Designing for Devanagari, Tamil, Bengali, Gujarati or any Indic script alongside Latin type is a distinctly different craft from adapting a Latin typeface with diacritics — stroke contrast, x-height matching across scripts, and vertical rhythm behave differently script to script. Independent Indian type foundries and studios working on multi-script systems (documented in `L8-design-04-typography.md`) are a directly usable reference set for EcoTrace, which will eventually need at least Hindi alongside English for a true pan-India SME audience.
- **Contemporary Indian design and craft-adjacent studios and platforms** worth following as primary sources rather than filtered through a Western trend feed: identity and packaging studios documented in the catalogue's branding and packaging categories, and the GI-registered artisan clusters named in Part 5 directly (searchable through India's GI Registry and state handicraft development corporations) — a more credible source than a generic "Indian pattern" asset pack.
- **The caution, restated from Part 5:** using Indian visual motifs without naming the specific tradition, region and, where commercial, without crediting or sourcing from the actual artisan clusters, is the difference between cross-pollination and extraction. A law student is better placed than most designers to understand why the GI Act's protections exist and what obligations, if any, attach to commercial use.

**Intuition anchors**
- The Eames Report treated craft as a design resource to build forward from, not a museum piece — the same stance this file takes throughout.
- "Trust badges are clutter" is a Western-market assumption; in a lower-trust market, they're load-bearing.

**Mastery checks**
- State what the Eames Report recommended and the year NID was founded as a result.
- Explain the low-trust vs high-trust design argument and why it changes what "clean" means as a goal.

**Analysis**
- Take one page from EcoTrace or a comparable Indian SME product and audit it for trust signals against the low-trust market argument. Note which trust signals are present, which are missing, and which (if any) feel like clutter rather than necessary reassurance — and justify the distinction.

**Taste**
- Designing well for the Indian market sometimes means resisting the instinct to "clean up" a page that is doing real work — the discipline is knowing which additions are trust and which are noise, not defaulting to either answer.

## PART 9 — Sameness: Pinterest, Dribbble & Algorithmic Taste [Core → Deep]

- **The diagnosis.** "Everyone pulls reference from the same three feeds, which is why everyone's work is starting to rhyme" (`TD-13#15`). This is not a claim about laziness or lack of imagination — it's closer to arithmetic. If a large share of a discipline's practitioners draw from the same small set of Pinterest boards, Dribbble shots and curated newsletters, convergence is the expected output, not a surprising failure.
- **Why Pinterest and Dribbble specifically produce this effect:**
  - Both platforms **rank by engagement**, which rewards whatever is already popular and visually loud enough to stop a scroll — a feedback loop that narrows the visible range of "good" work over time rather than widening it.
  - Dribbble shots in particular are optimised for the thumbnail and the scroll, not for the working interface: gradients, glows and stacked translucent layers ("layering," `TD-13#30`) read as impressive at thumbnail size in a way that doesn't always survive contact with real content, real states and real users.
  - Neither platform is an archive in the Part 2 sense — there's no enforced credit, no historical depth, and the ranking algorithm actively suppresses older or less-engaged work regardless of quality.
- **The structural fix, not a motivational one:** change your inputs, deliberately.
  - **Train your algorithm once, deliberately** — curating who you follow is a one-hour investment that changes every subsequent day of input; a feed you didn't configure is a feed someone else configured for you (`TD-13#17`).
  - **Consume off-algorithm** — deliberately choose sources with zero apparent relevance to your work (a different medium, a different era, a different discipline entirely), the same move as Steve Jobs and calligraphy (`TD-13#11` habit 8).
  - **Prefer archives to galleries** for anything you intend to reference directly, not just browse (Part 2).
  - **Question whether a moodboard is collecting or absorbing** before you build one — it's frequently the former dressed as the latter (`TD-13#25`).
- **Algorithmic taste is now compounding with generative AI.** When a model is trained on the internet's current visual output, and that output has already converged toward a narrow, engagement-optimised aesthetic, AI-generated interfaces tend to reproduce and amplify the same narrow range — more "layering," more of the same gradient treatments — unless a designer deliberately steers away from the model's defaults. This is the practical, everyday version of the evaluation-is-the-scarce-skill argument in this file's context stamp: knowing what the model's defaults look like, and choosing not to accept them uncritically, is now a taste skill in its own right (`L8-design-30-generative-ai-assisted.md` covers the mechanics).
- **A trend is not automatically bad, and sameness is not automatically avoidable — the goal is deliberateness, not contrarianism for its own sake.** Using a currently popular pattern because you evaluated it against your product's needs and it won is different from using it because it was the fifth Dribbble shot you scrolled past today. Part 10 develops this distinction fully.

**Intuition anchors**
- Convergence from shared feeds is arithmetic, not a personal failure of imagination — which means the fix is structural (change the inputs), not motivational (try harder to be original).
- A Dribbble shot is optimised to stop a thumbnail scroll; a shipped interface is optimised to survive real content and real users. They are not the same design problem.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain why Pinterest and Dribbble structurally produce sameness, distinct from any individual designer's laziness.
- State two structural (not motivational) fixes for algorithmic sameness.
- Explain how generative AI can compound the sameness problem and what a designer can do about it.

**Analysis**
- Audit your own reference sources for the last month: list every place you pulled inspiration from. Classify each as archive or gallery/feed. If the list is mostly feed, name one archive source you'll add this week.

**Taste**
- The designers whose work doesn't rhyme with everyone else's are, almost without exception, the ones whose inputs don't either.

## PART 10 — Taste vs Trend, and Taste in Product Decisions: What You Leave Out [Core → Deep]

- **Taste and trend are frequently confused, and the distinction is decidable.** A trend is a pattern currently popular because many people are copying it (layering, glassmorphism, a particular gradient treatment, this season's Dribbble aesthetic). Taste is the judgement that decides *whether a given trend serves this product, for this audience, right now* — and taste can validly say yes. The test is not "is this trendy," it's "did I choose this because I evaluated it, or because it's what's currently everywhere." A trend adopted after evaluation is a design decision; a trend adopted by default is decoration.
  - **Trends date; taste-driven decisions age instead.** A trend-chased interface looks dated the moment the trend turns over, because nothing about the choice was tied to the product's actual constraints. A taste-driven decision — even one that happens to use a currently popular pattern — ages, because the reasoning behind it (why this serves this user, this content, this brand) doesn't expire when the aesthetic does.
- **The core act of taste in a product decision is deciding what to leave out.** Anyone can add a feature, a gradient, an animation, a fourth trust badge. Taste is the discipline of removing the fifth one because it no longer earns its place — the same "kill your darlings" discipline from Part 2, applied to shipped product rather than a single artefact.
- **Worked decision — EcoTrace, an ESG/BRSR compliance dashboard for Indian SMEs.**
  - **Options considered** for the compliance-score summary view: (a) a dense data-table-first layout showing every underlying metric at once, matching how most compliance software presents itself; (b) a single hero score with a simplified traffic-light status and drill-down on demand; (c) a narrative summary in plain language ("your BRSR filing is 80% complete; three items need attention") with the data available but not default.
  - **Criteria:** the primary user is often a time-pressed SME owner or a junior compliance staffer, not a full-time ESG analyst; the product's credibility depends on being trusted with a regulatory filing, so it cannot look under-built; and the target market's trust-signal norm (Part 8) means "clean minimalism with nothing to inspect" would itself read as untrustworthy for a compliance product specifically, even though it might work for a consumer app.
  - **Choice:** a hybrid — option (c)'s plain-language framing as the default view, with option (a)'s full data table one click away, and option (b)'s status colour used sparingly as a single accent, not the whole interface. What was *left out*: a fully dense dashboard as the default (rejected as overwhelming for the primary user), and a fully narrative-only view with no drill-down (rejected because it would fail the trust-signal expectation of a regulatory product — an SME compliance officer needs to be able to verify the number, not just be told it).
  - **Consequence:** the taste judgement here was not "make it beautiful," it was "match the default view to who actually opens this screen under time pressure, while keeping the underlying data one click from anyone who needs to audit it." That's a testable claim (Part 11), not an aesthetic preference.
- **Worked decision — Meridian UI, a React component library.**
  - **Options considered** for the design-token naming convention: (a) purely semantic names (`color-danger`, `spacing-compact`) with no reference to raw values; (b) purely primitive/scale names (`red-500`, `space-2`) exposed directly to consumers; (c) a two-tier system — primitive tokens as the internal source of truth, semantic tokens as the only public API.
  - **Criteria:** Meridian is consumed by other engineers, some building products very different from EcoTrace, so the naming has to be legible to someone with zero context on the original design decisions; it also has to survive a rebrand or theme change without every consumer's code breaking.
  - **Choice:** option (c). What was left out: exposing primitive names directly to consumers (rejected — it ties every consuming product's code to Meridian's internal colour scale, so a rebrand breaks everyone) and a purely semantic system with no documented primitive layer underneath (rejected — it hides the reasoning from anyone extending the system later, which is exactly the "kill your darlings but keep the documentation" failure).
  - **Consequence:** the constraint (two tiers, semantic-only public API) is more work upfront than either single-tier option, and that extra discipline is the taste decision — restraint applied to an API surface, not a colour palette, which is the same judgement Part 3's fashion-collection discipline describes, wearing engineering clothes.
- **A quick test for whether a decision was taste or trend-following:** can you state, without referencing what's currently popular, why this choice serves this specific user and this specific content? If the honest answer references only "this is what good products look like right now," it's trend-following. If it references the actual user, task and constraint, it's taste — even if it happens to look current.

**Intuition anchors**
- A trend adopted after evaluation ages like a decision; a trend adopted by default ages like a receipt.
- The taste question in a product review is never "does this look good," it's "what did we choose to leave out, and why."

**Mastery checks**
- State the decidable test for distinguishing taste from trend-following.
- Walk through the EcoTrace summary-view decision: options, criteria, choice, what was left out, and the testable consequence.
- Walk through the Meridian token-naming decision the same way.

**Analysis**
- Pick one decision in your own current work (EcoTrace, Meridian UI, or a personal project) where you added something. Rewrite it as an options → criteria → choice → consequence structure, and identify explicitly what you chose to leave out and why.

**Taste**
- The best compliment a product decision can earn isn't "it looks great," it's "of course it works that way" — which only happens when what's missing was removed on purpose.

## PART 11 — How Users Perceive "Quality": Evidence on Aesthetics & Perceived Usability [Core → Deep]

- **The aesthetic–usability effect, named and sourced correctly.** In 1995, Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura published "Apparent Usability vs. Inherent Usability: Experimental Analysis on the Determinants of the Apparent Usability" at CHI '95 (ACM). Testing variations of an ATM-style interface, they found that **how usable an interface appeared to users correlated more strongly with how attractive it looked than with how usable it actually was when tested.** This became known as the **aesthetic–usability effect**: attractive things are perceived as more usable, whether or not they actually are.
- **The nuance, which the headline version usually drops:**
  - **The effect describes *perceived* usability, not actual task performance.** A beautiful interface can still fail a user at the task; it just takes longer for the user to notice or admit it, and they'll rate the experience more forgivingly along the way. Confusing "felt easy" with "was easy" is the exact mistake the aesthetic–usability effect describes, and it's just as easy for a designer to make about their own work as for a user to make about a product.
  - **The effect is strongest before use and weakens with sustained, high-stakes use.** First impressions and low-stakes tasks are where beauty buys the most forgiveness; a user filing a real compliance return under deadline pressure (EcoTrace's context, Part 10) is far less forgiving of a beautiful interface that's actually slow or confusing than a first-time browser of a marketing site is.
  - **The effect can mask real usability problems during testing**, which is a methodological trap: users in a study will often rate a good-looking prototype as "easy to use" even when observed task completion is poor, because their stated satisfaction and their measured performance are answering different questions. This is why Nielsen Norman Group and other usability-research bodies caution against relying on self-reported ease-of-use scores alone — task completion rate, time-on-task and error count have to be measured independently of how attractive the interface looks, precisely because attractiveness biases the self-report.
  - **Beauty is not free of context** — the "MAYA principle" ("Most Advanced Yet Acceptable," from industrial designer Raymond Loewy) suggests users respond best to the most advanced, most refined version of something that is still recognisable to them; a beautiful interface that breaks too many conventions at once can read as *unfamiliar* rather than *high-quality*, which cuts against the aesthetic–usability effect rather than reinforcing it. Beauty helps most when it's legible, not merely novel.
- **What this means for testing (not just designing):**
  - **Separate satisfaction metrics from performance metrics in every usability test.** Ask "how easy was that" and also measure task success, time and errors — and report both, because they will sometimes disagree, and the disagreement is the useful finding.
  - **Test under realistic stakes, not just first impressions**, especially for a product like EcoTrace where the real user is under regulatory time pressure, not casually browsing. A five-second first-impression test tells you about the aesthetic–usability effect's strongest zone; a full-task usability session under time pressure tells you whether the beauty survives contact with the actual job.
  - **Watch for the effect working against you in critique.** A stakeholder or a designer's own eye can rate a polished mockup as "clearly more usable" than a rougher but better-tested alternative, purely because of the aesthetic–usability effect — which is exactly why usability testing with real tasks, not just visual review, has to be the final check on a taste judgement (`L8-design-02` Part 9 covers the broader evidence base for principles; this Part is the aesthetics-specific case of it).
- **The honest summary:** beauty buys real, measured goodwill — more patience with minor flaws, higher satisfaction ratings, more forgiveness of small errors — and that goodwill is worth having. It does not buy actual task success, and treating it as though it does is the single most common way a beautiful redesign quietly makes a product harder to use while everyone in the room feels good about it.

**Intuition anchors**
- Beauty buys patience, not competence — and patience runs out under real deadline pressure.
- If a usability test's satisfaction score and its task-completion rate disagree, believe the task-completion rate.

**Mastery checks**
- State the aesthetic–usability effect and its source (author, year, venue) accurately.
- Explain the difference between perceived and actual usability, and why a usability test needs to measure both separately.
- Explain the MAYA principle and how it qualifies, rather than simply confirms, the aesthetic–usability effect.

**Analysis**
- Design or find two versions of the same simple task flow — one visually polished, one visually plain but identically functional. Run a small informal test (even five people) measuring both self-reported ease and actual task completion time. Note any disagreement between the two measures.

**Taste**
- Trusting your own eye that a design "feels" more usable is exactly the bias this Part describes — the discipline is testing the claim, not just noticing that you believe it.

## PART 12 — Masters, Connections & Taste: A Lifelong Practice [Lifelong]

- **There is no finishing point.** Every mechanism in Part 1 (exposure, comparison, articulation, making) has to keep running for as long as you design, because the inputs around you keep changing — new trends, new tools, new adjacent fields worth raiding. Taste that stops updating calcifies into house style whether or not you notice.
- **A rotation, not a single reading list, is the sustainable form this takes.** Rotate which adjacent field (Parts 3–7) you're actively drawing from every few months rather than settling permanently on one; a designer who has spent a year reading only about typography and a designer who has spent a year reading only about architecture will both plateau, for the same reason — one input, however deep, is still one input.
- **Revisit your own past work on a schedule**, not by accident. Work that doesn't slightly embarrass you after six months either means you've stopped growing or you're not looking closely enough (Part 0's gap argument, run in reverse: the gap should be widening ahead of you even as it closes behind you).
- **Masters worth studying as complete practices, not single quotes:** Dieter Rams (read with the Part 0 caveat — one tradition, not a universal law), the Eames office (Part 8, and their own cross-pollination between furniture, film and exhibition design), Pentagram's partners (Part 2, for documented rationale rather than just outcomes), and — deliberately outside design — anyone whose non-design practice you've raided in Parts 3–7 for a principle that transferred.
- **Connections across this curriculum:** this file is the synthesis layer for `L8-design-01` (mindset, craft/judgement/taste, Part 3 and Part 12), `L8-design-02` (visual principles, worked decisions and evidence, Part 8 and Part 9), and `L8-design-08` (history and Indian design history, Part 8 and Part 11) — read together, those three plus this file are the closest thing this curriculum has to a taste core. `L8-design-31-ethics-philosophy.md` extends Part 5 and Part 8's borrowing caveats into a full ethical framework. `L8-design-30-generative-ai-assisted.md` extends Part 9's algorithmic-sameness argument into the mechanics of working with generative tools without losing your own eye.
- **The closing standard for this whole file:** taste is judged by what you produce and what you chose not to, tested against real users under real conditions — not by how well you can talk about it. Every Part in this file exists to make the next thing you ship better, not to make the next conversation about design sound smarter.

**Intuition anchors**
- One input, however deep, plateaus. Rotation is what keeps taste developing rather than calcifying.
- If your six-month-old work doesn't embarrass you slightly, check whether you're actually looking.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain why a rotation across adjacent fields outperforms depth in a single field, for taste development specifically.
- Name the three files (besides this one) that form this curriculum's taste core, and what each contributes.

**Analysis**
- Set a recurring quarterly note: which adjacent field will you deliberately draw from next quarter, and what is the specific exercise (from Parts 3–7) you'll run.

**Taste**
- The designers worth calling masters are rarely the ones with the most confident opinions — they're the ones still visibly updating their taste in public, shipping work that shows it.
