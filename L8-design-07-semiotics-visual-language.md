## How To Use This Document

**Depth ladder:** **L1** Name it · **L2** Use it correctly · **L3** Choose it over alternatives · **L4** Explain the mechanism · **L5** Build a sign system from scratch · **L6** Teach it and critique misuse.

**Tier tags:** **[Core]** every designer · **[Deep]** senior depth · **[Frontier]** specialist or moving.

**Each Part closes with:** *Intuition anchors* · *Mastery checks* · *Analysis* · *Taste*.

**The semiotics-specific learning loop:**
1. **Read everything twice:** once for what it *shows* (denotation), once for what it *suggests* (connotation). Do it with ads, packaging, app icons, government forms, temple signage and your own work.
2. **Ask "compared to what?"** Signs mean through difference (Saussure). A serif means "traditional" only against a field of sans; a pink means "feminine" only inside a code that assigns it. Always map the field before judging a sign.
3. **Find the reader.** Meaning happens in a reader. For every design, name at least two audiences who would read it differently, and test with both.
4. **Collect sign systems, not images.** Pictogram sets, flag systems, road signs, emoji, mudras, hallmarks, railway liveries. Study how a *system* keeps its members distinct yet related.
5. **Name codes precisely.** "Premium" is vague. "Matte black, generous margins, a didone wordmark, one metallic accent" is a code you can use, subvert or avoid (`TD-06#15 · @cheatdaydesign · DKkqSiSyB0V` names health-washing grammar exactly like this).
6. **Test meaning, never assume it.** Icon comprehension tests, semantic-differential ratings and 5-second "what is this?" tests (T8) turn semiotics from opinion into evidence.

## PART 0 — Foundations: What a Sign Is [Core]

- **Semiotics** (or semiology) is the study of signs: anything that stands for something else to someone. Design is professional sign-making. Every logo, icon, colour choice, typeface, photograph, layout and animation is read as meaning something, whether or not you intended it. **You can't design meaning-free. You can only design meaning on purpose or by accident.**
- **Saussure's dyadic sign** (Ferdinand de Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics*, published posthumously 1916):
  - A sign = **signifier** (the form: a sound, a shape, a word) + **signified** (the concept it evokes).
  - The link is **arbitrary**: nothing about the word "tree" resembles a tree.
  - Meaning comes from **difference within a system**. "Red" means stop partly because green means go.
  - Design lessons: a visual language means through *contrasts within itself*, and a sign changes meaning when its system changes.
- **Peirce's triadic sign** (Charles Sanders Peirce, 1860s–1900s): a **representamen** (the sign vehicle) stands for an **object** and produces an **interpretant** (the understanding in a mind). Peirce's most useful gift to designers is the classification of how signs relate to what they stand for: **icon, index, symbol** (Part 1).
- **Denotation and connotation** (Roland Barthes, *Elements of Semiology*, 1964; *Mythologies*, 1957): **denotation** is the literal, first-order meaning (a photo shows a woman drinking tea). **Connotation** is the second-order, culturally coded meaning (calm, morning ritual, Britishness, luxury, depending on how it's shot). **Myth** is Barthes' term for connotations that have become so naturalised they feel like common sense (a flag on a product = "authentic, patriotic"). Design operates mostly at the level of connotation.
- **Syntactics, semantics, pragmatics** (Charles Morris, 1938): **syntactics** is the relations between signs (grammar, layout, composition), **semantics** is the relations between signs and meanings, **pragmatics** is the relations between signs and their users in context. Massimo Vignelli built *The Vignelli Canon* on exactly this triad. **Every design critique can be sorted into these three questions:** is it well-formed? Does it mean the right thing? Does it work for these people here?
- **Why designers need the vocabulary:** it turns "it feels cheap" into an analysable claim ("the stock-photo code of 'diverse team smiling at laptop' connotes generic corporate, contradicting the brand's claim to craft") and makes deliberate meaning-making teachable.

**Intuition anchors**
- A design doesn't *have* a meaning. It *gets* one from a reader, in a context, against everything else they've seen.
- Most design happens at the level of connotation, so learn to see the second meaning as clearly as the first.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain Saussure's signifier/signified, arbitrariness and "meaning through difference", with a design example of each.
- Distinguish denotation, connotation and myth using one advertisement.
- Explain Morris's syntactics, semantics and pragmatics, and sort three critique notes into them.

**Analysis**
- Take one Indian print ad (an Amul topical, a Fevicol ad, a Cadbury festive ad). Write its denotation (what is literally shown and written), its connotations (at least five) and the "myth" it relies on. 300 words.

**Taste**
- Always ask what your design says beyond what it shows. The brief you were given is the denotation. The work that lasts gets the connotation right.

## PART 1 — Icon, Index, Symbol [Core]

- **Peirce's three modes**, the most practical tool in the module:
  - **Icon:** resembles its object (a drawing of a house for "home", a camera silhouette for "photo", a map). It's learned fast and depends on the reader recognising the resemblance.
  - **Index:** is *causally or physically connected* to its object (smoke → fire, a footprint → a walker, a progress bar → a process running, a typing indicator → someone typing, a notification dot → unseen activity, a pulsing record button → recording in progress). Indices are powerful in interfaces because they **show that something is happening**.
  - **Symbol:** a *conventional*, learned link (the word "save", the heart for "like", the hamburger ≡ for "menu", the @ sign, a red cross for medical aid). It's arbitrary and works only for people who've learned it.

  Most real signs mix modes: a floppy-disk "save" icon *was* iconic (it resembled the medium) and is now **purely symbolic** for anyone under 25.
- **Interface examples, classified:**
  - **Iconic:** trash can, envelope, magnifying glass (partly), play triangle (via the tape-deck convention).
  - **Indexical:** spinners, skeleton screens, cursor changes, read receipts, typing dots, a battery level, a download ring filling.
  - **Symbolic:** hamburger ≡ (Norm Cox, Xerox Star, 1981), kebab ⋮, gear for settings (partly iconic), share icons (different on iOS and Android), the ✨ sparkle for AI (a symbol being learned right now).
- **Why some icons are "universal" and others fail:** universality comes from wide learning, not from resemblance. The magnifier for search, ✕ for close and the house for home are learned across platforms. Novel or platform-specific icons (share, sync, archive, the "more" variants) fail without labels. **NN/g's long-standing guidance: icons need text labels except for a handful of truly standard ones.** Users often can't reliably guess the meaning of an unlabelled icon, even a common one, in an unfamiliar context.
- **Skeuomorphism as iconicity:** skeuomorphs borrow the form of a physical predecessor (stitched leather calendars, notepad textures, a camera shutter sound on phones). Apple's 2013 iOS 7 flattening was a **semiotic shift**: once users had learned the symbols, iconic resemblance could be dropped. Every skeuomorph has a lifecycle: iconic → conventional → dead metaphor (the floppy, the phone handset for "call", a telephone shape almost nobody under 20 has used).
- **Index and trust:** indexical signs are hard to fake credibly, which makes them trustworthy. A real progress bar showing actual progress beats a fake one. A **timestamp and a green "verified" tick** work because they point to a process. Faking indices (fake "12 people viewing", fake progress) is the semiotics of dark patterns (06 Part 6).
- **An index is only as honest as the process behind it.** A typing indicator that shows while a chatbot waits for a server, rather than while anything is being written, borrows the credibility of a human sign for a machine delay. It isn't illegal, but it teaches users that your indices are theatre. Part 11 applies this to verification ticks, badges and compliance status.

**Intuition anchors**
- If an icon needs you to have lived through its object (floppies, phone handsets, film cameras), it's already a symbol. Treat it as something users must learn, and label it.
- Indices are the honest signs of an interface: they show that something real is happening. Never fake them.

**Mastery checks**
- Classify 15 UI signs as icon, index or symbol (or mixed), with reasons.
- Explain the floppy-disk lifecycle as a semiotic process.
- Explain why the hamburger menu works for some audiences and fails for others.
- Explain why faked indices are a trust violation.

**Analysis**
- Screenshot the tab bars and toolbars of five apps (including one Indian government app such as UMANG or DigiLocker). Classify every icon as icon/index/symbol, mark which are labelled, and rate the unlabelled ones for comprehension risk. Redesign the riskiest bar *[Figma: Yellow]*.

**Taste**
- Label your symbols. Designers love the purity of icon-only toolbars. Users love knowing what a button does, and only one of them is paying for the product.

## PART 2 — Codes, Culture & Readers [Core → Deep]

- **Codes:** systems of conventions that let signs be read (Umberto Eco, *A Theory of Semiotics*, 1976). Visual codes include genre codes (what a horror poster looks like), category codes (what a bank website looks like), cultural codes (what white means at a wedding) and medium codes (what a button looks like). **Designers work by using, bending or breaking codes.** Using a code gives instant recognition, breaking it gives distinctiveness, and the craft is choosing which codes to keep. Colour and type "psychology" posts (blue = trust, serif = authority) are really lists of **learned codes in a particular market**, useful as conventions and wrong as universal laws (`TD-04#31 · @howentrepreneursthinks · C6bVN_9PIfl`; `TD-03#29 · @reputeforge · C9U43CeSuZ0`).
- **Encoding and decoding** (Stuart Hall, *Encoding/Decoding*, 1973): producers encode meanings, but audiences decode them from their own positions. The **dominant** reading accepts the intended meaning, the **negotiated** reading accepts some and adapts some, and the **oppositional** reading rejects it and reads against it. A campaign designed for a dominant reading can be read oppositionally by a community it offends. Plan for all three.
- **Cultural variation, with concrete examples to memorise:**
  - **Gestures:** thumbs-up is approval in India and the US but offensive in parts of West Africa and the Middle East. The "OK" ring gesture is rude in Brazil and was co-opted as a hate symbol online in some contexts.
  - **Marks:** in Japan a circle (○, *maru*) marks "correct" and a check can mark an error. In some Scandinavian school traditions a ✓ has also marked mistakes. The universal "✓ = yes" is a Western convention.
  - **Animals:** the owl is wisdom in Western iconography (and Lakshmi's vahana in Hindu iconography, signifying prosperity) but a bad omen or a fool ("ullu") in everyday North Indian usage. Cows, elephants and snakes all carry region-specific sacred meanings.
  - **Religious and political symbols:** the **swastika** (svastika) is an ancient auspicious symbol in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, seen on Indian doorways and account books, and the emblem of Nazism in the West. Any global product with Indian festive imagery must handle this deliberately.
  - **Colour:** see 03 Part 4 (white, red, saffron, green, black, and red-is-up in Chinese markets).
  - **Reading direction:** a "before → after" image sequence reads in reverse for RTL readers. A famous (possibly apocryphal) anecdote about a detergent ad in the Middle East makes the point: dirty → soap → clean became clean → soap → dirty.
  - **Iconic objects:** mailbox icons (the US rural mailbox means nothing in India), a "shopping cart" vs a "bag" or "basket", dollar-sign defaults for money, the letter-envelope for messages.
- **Category codes and the field** (bridge to 10): each product category develops a visual dialect (fintech: blue, geometric sans, abstract gradients, smiling-person photography; Indian FMCG: saturated colour, dense packaging, celebrity faces; D2C wellness: muted earth tones, serif-plus-sans, lifestyle flat-lays). Mapping the category code is the first step of positioning. **Obvious category cues are where everyone already is. Map them, then leave** (`TD-06#4 · @cj.cawley.design · DctOAYwMi9e`).
- **Residual, dominant and emergent codes** (Raymond Williams, *Marxism and Literature*, 1977, adapted by brand semioticians like Virginia Valentine): every category has **residual** codes (old, fading, nostalgic), **dominant** codes (current mainstream) and **emergent** codes (new, rising, signalling the future). Examples: anti-polish and handmade as emergent in response to AI (`TD-06#5 · @ryanbailey.cb · DbZDnSpyilk`; `TD-07#25`), "minimal on the shelf" turning into sameness (`TD-12#18 · @the_0rangespace_ · DFxQ1dnMd_I`), and health-washing's matte-dark-functional grammar as dominant in wellness (`TD-06#15`). **Designing with emergent codes positions a brand as ahead. Leaning on residual codes positions it as heritage or kitsch, and either can be right.**
- **Intertextuality and pastiche:** signs borrow from other signs (a poster quoting a film, a UI referencing a 1984 Mac, period-style redraws of modern logos, `TD-06#20 · @kxdgraphics · DLKE_RaNgXp`). **Quotation** that is knowing and transformed builds meaning, and **pastiche** that is unknowing and surface-level reads as derivative.

**Intuition anchors**
- There's no universal visual language, only widely shared codes. Always ask whose code you're using.
- The category's dominant code is the fastest way to be understood and the fastest way to be forgotten.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain Hall's three reading positions with a campaign that was read oppositionally.
- Give eight cross-cultural sign differences relevant to Indian and global products.
- Explain residual, dominant and emergent codes with one category example of each.
- Map the dominant code of one category in five concrete attributes.

**Analysis**
- Choose one Indian category (UPI apps, ayurvedic skincare, D2C coffee, coaching institutes). Screenshot 10 brands. Build a code map in FigJam *[Figma: White]*: residual, dominant and emergent codes across colour, type, imagery, language and layout. Mark the white space where no brand currently sits.

**Taste**
- Learn the codes so well that breaking one is a decision. The most memorable identities keep just enough of the category's code to be understood and break exactly one to be remembered.

## PART 3 — Visual Rhetoric: Making Images Argue [Core → Deep]

- **Rhetoric is persuasion through form.** Classical rhetoric's figures have visual equivalents. Barthes' *Rhetoric of the Image* (1964) analysed a Panzani pasta ad into a **linguistic message**, a **denoted image** and a **connoted image** ("Italianicity"), and named two text–image relations: **anchorage** (text fixes which meaning of the image to take, as in a caption) and **relay** (text and image carry different parts of the message, as in comics). Almost every poster and ad you'll make is an anchorage problem.
- **The core visual figures:**
  - **Metaphor:** one thing shown as another to transfer qualities (a bank as a solid rock, spaghetti packaged as hair, `TD-12#6 · @reputeforge · DbVqcfcoFqV`).
  - **Metonymy:** something associated stands for the whole (a stethoscope for medicine, a crown for royalty, a gavel for law).
  - **Synecdoche:** a part stands for the whole (one hand holding a phone = a user).
  - **Juxtaposition and contrast:** two images side by side force a comparison (before/after, "the image vs the design", `TD-07#49 · @marlocube · DBMOiBSlShy`).
  - **Visual pun and rebus:** Paul Rand's **Eye-Bee-M** poster for IBM (1981) (`TD-07#26 · @dominicbydesign`: strong identity systems can afford playful variants).
  - **Hyperbole and scale shift:** an astronaut in moss (`TD-07#47 · @foxedition_fe`).
  - **Irony:** image contradicts text.
  - **Personification:** products with faces (06 Part 5 baby schema).
  - **Visual paradox and impossible objects.**
  - **Shadow and negative space as narrative:** the shadow says what the object cannot (`TD-07#37 · @ginyboi`).
  - **Pattern breaking:** one figure out of line in a grid, where the composition *is* the message (`TD-07#49`).
- **McQuarrie & Mick's typology** (1996; visual version 1999): rhetorical figures are either **schemes** (surface patterning: repetition, rhyme, reversal) or **tropes** (deviations of meaning: metaphor, pun, irony). Tropes are more complex and, in studies, more engaging and memorable **if the audience can decode them**. Too complex and they fail. Match rhetorical complexity to the audience's time and literacy: a billboard gets one clear trope, a magazine spread can afford a layered one.
- **Concept-first design:** the strongest work carries meaning in its *behaviour* or *form*, not in decoration. Examples: a tearable divorce card, a surveillance-camera *1984* cover, packaging that becomes a second object (`TD-07#7 · @inavinate`; `TD-12#1 · @reputeforge · DctR7FHIASS`). Oki Sato's test for nendo: can the idea be drawn as a **five-line diagram**? (`TD-12#5 · @taralaradesign · DbV_0RboBve`).
- **Masters of visual rhetoric:**
  - **Bill Bernbach and DDB:** VW's *Think Small* (1959), self-deprecating irony and white space.
  - **Paul Rand:** wit and rebus.
  - **Shigeo Fukuda:** optical paradox, political posters.
  - **Milton Glaser:** *I ♥ NY* (1977), a rebus with a heart as a symbol of love.
  - **Abram Games:** "maximum meaning, minimum means".
  - **Polish poster school:** Henryk Tomaszewski, Jan Lenica. Metaphor under censorship.
  - **Amul's topical ads:** since 1966, Sylvester daCunha and Eustace Fernandes' "Utterly Butterly" girl, commenting on the news with puns for nearly 60 years. The longest-running visual rhetoric campaign you can study daily.
- **Ethics of rhetoric:** persuasion becomes manipulation when figures mislead about facts (health-washing, fake scarcity, doctored before/after images). ASCI (the Advertising Standards Council of India) codes and consumer-protection law apply to visual claims too (bridge to 31).

**Intuition anchors**
- A strong visual idea can be described in one sentence and drawn in five lines. If yours needs a paragraph, it's decoration.
- The text decides which meaning of the image counts. Write captions as carefully as you compose images.

**Mastery checks**
- Distinguish anchorage and relay with examples.
- Define and give a design example of metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, juxtaposition, rebus and irony.
- Explain McQuarrie & Mick's scheme/trope distinction and the complexity trade-off.
- Apply Oki Sato's five-line test to three of your own concepts.

**Analysis**
- Study 20 Amul topical hoardings (the Amul website archive). Classify the rhetorical figure in each, and note how the pun anchors the image. Then design three topicals of your own for this week's news *[Figma: White]*, in Amul's code and deliberately outside it.

**Taste**
- Aim for one idea per piece, stated with the fewest possible means. Visual wit is valuable because it makes the viewer do a small, pleasurable piece of work, and more than one idea makes that work a chore.

## PART 4 — Pictograms, Wayfinding & Sign Systems [Core → Deep]

- **Isotype:** Otto Neurath with illustrator Gerd Arntz and Marie Neurath (Vienna, 1925–34; then The Hague and Oxford) created the **International System of Typographic Picture Education**: standardised pictograms to communicate social and economic data to anyone, regardless of literacy. Principles still valid: **one symbol = one fixed quantity**, repeat rather than enlarge to show more, keep the pictorial style consistent, reduce to essentials. It's the ancestor of infographics, pictogram systems and much of 22.
- **Olympic and transport pictograms:** Masaru Katsumie and Yoshiro Yamashita's Tokyo 1964 sports pictograms; **Otl Aicher's Munich 1972** system (strict geometric grid, 45°/90° angles, the template for modern pictogram design); the **AIGA/US DOT symbol signs** (1974–79, designed by Roger Cook and Don Shanosky, 50 symbols, public domain, the airport icons you know). The Tokyo 2020 Olympics introduced **kinetic pictograms** (and the opening ceremony performed them live).
- **Standards:** **ISO 7001** (public information symbols), **ISO 7010** (safety signs), **ISO 3864** (safety colours and shapes: red circle = prohibition, yellow triangle = warning, blue circle = mandatory, green square = safe condition). **ISO 9186** defines comprehension testing, with high acceptance criteria (on the order of two-thirds correct for public information and far higher for safety symbols). **Shape and colour are coded before content**: a red circle means "no" before you see what's inside it.
- **Designing a pictogram family:**
  - A **grid and construction rules:** stroke weight, corner treatment, angles, a "live area".
  - A consistent **viewpoint** (profile vs frontal) and **level of abstraction**.
  - **Distinct silhouettes** so members don't confuse at small sizes.
  - A **figure** convention (the "ISO man" and its gender and diversity debates).
  - **Testing with target users**, especially across literacy levels and cultures.

  Full craft in 11. Here the point is meaning: **a pictogram system is a language with a vocabulary and a grammar**, and consistency is what makes it readable.
- **Wayfinding:** Kevin Lynch's *The Image of the City* (1960) identified **paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks** as the elements people use to form mental maps. Wayfinding design (airports, hospitals, metro systems, campuses, and digital navigation) supplies **identification, direction, orientation and regulation** signs at decision points. Indian examples to study: Delhi Metro's colour-coded lines and trilingual signage, Indian Railways' station boards (yellow with black text in Hindi, English and the regional language), Mumbai's BEST bus route numbering. Digital analogues: breadcrumbs (paths), section headers (districts), home and search (landmarks).
- **Emoji as a living sign system:** Shigetaka Kurita's 176 emoji for NTT Docomo (1999) → Unicode standardisation (2010) → thousands today. Lessons: **the same code point renders differently across platforms**, which changes meaning (Hannah Miller et al., 2016, found people often disagree on an emoji's sentiment, especially across platforms); meanings drift culturally (🙏 as prayer, thanks, high-five or namaste); new signs get added annually through a public proposal process. Emoji are a live case study in conventional symbols being negotiated in public.

**Intuition anchors**
- A pictogram isn't a picture. It's a word in a visual language, readable only because the rest of the language is consistent.
- Shape and colour are read before the content. Get the safety code right and the icon inside becomes confirmation, not the whole message.

**Mastery checks**
- State Isotype's core rules and how they survive in infographics today.
- Explain the ISO 3864 shape and colour code.
- List Lynch's five city elements and map them onto a mobile app's navigation.
- Explain why the same emoji can be read differently across platforms.

**Analysis**
- Walk your campus or the Tiruchirappalli Junction railway station and photograph every wayfinding sign on one route (entrance to a platform or a hostel). Map the decision points. Note missing, ambiguous or contradictory signs. Propose a pictogram-plus-trilingual sign family for three signs *[Figma: Yellow]*.

**Taste**
- Design sign systems for the most tired, least literate, most hurried person who will ever see them: a grandparent at a station at 5 a.m. with a train leaving. Systems that serve them serve everyone.

## PART 5 — Visual Languages & Style Systems [Core → Deep]

- **A visual language** is a coherent set of signs (**vocabulary**: shapes, colours, type, image style, icon style, motion, texture) with rules for combining them (**grammar**: grid, hierarchy, proportions, spacing, when each element appears). A brand identity, a design system, an Olympic look and a publication's art direction are all visual languages. **Recognition comes from the grammar more than from any single element**: you recognise a Wes Anderson frame or a Swiss poster from how elements combine.
- **Style vocabulary as professional literacy:** name styles precisely: movements (Bauhaus, Swiss, Art Deco, Memphis, Brutalism), internet-era aesthetics (Y2K, vaporwave, neo-brutalism, glassmorphism, claymorphism) and coined combinations. Useful references: the 29 named styles (`TD-07#11 · @designbyanishka`), the 2026 trend board (`TD-07#3 · @expert.pitch`), and **coined style names** built from [subject/mood] + [technique/treatment], like "Desert Monoline" or "Architectural Duotone" (`TD-07#19 · @createwithalena`). **Naming a style lets you brief it, critique it and deliberately avoid it.** Movement names also work as AI prompts and search terms (`TD-07#6 · @elicoleman_`; `TD-07#18 · @designsubway`).
- **Interface design languages as semiotic systems:**
  - **Skeuomorphism** (pre-2013 iOS): material metaphors.
  - **Flat** (Windows Metro 2010, iOS 7 2013): symbols stripped of material.
  - **Material Design** (Google, 2014): paper-and-ink physics as a metaphor.
  - **Neumorphism** (circa 2019–20): extruded soft shadows, poor for contrast and short-lived.
  - **Glassmorphism** and Apple's **Liquid Glass** (2025): translucency as depth and system presence.

  Each is a set of codes that users learn and later read as "era". Know them so you choose an era deliberately rather than inherit one.
- **Meaning through difference, applied** (Saussure again): a visual language works when its members are **distinct enough to differ and similar enough to belong**. In a design system, *primary* vs *secondary* button must differ clearly; icons in a set must share stroke and grid; brand colours must be distinguishable in greyscale and to CVD viewers (03). **When two signs in a system are too similar, one of them means nothing.**
- **Measuring connotation:** Charles Osgood's **semantic differential** (*The Measurement of Meaning*, 1957): people rate a stimulus on bipolar scales (modern–traditional, warm–cold, cheap–premium, playful–serious). Factor analysis found three main dimensions: **evaluation** (good–bad), **potency** (strong–weak) and **activity** (active–passive). It's cheap, powerful and underused. Rate your logo, typeface or palette on 6–8 scales with 10 people and compare against the brief's intended profile (T8 exercises).
- **Visual grammar of composition** (Gunther Kress & Theo van Leeuwen, *Reading Images: The Grammar of Visual Design*, 1996; 3rd ed. 2021):
  - **Given/new:** left = already known, right = new or the message, in LTR cultures.
  - **Ideal/real:** top = aspiration or promise, bottom = factual detail or action.
  - **Centre/margin:** centre = core, margins = subordinate.
  - **Salience:** what draws the eye.
  - **Framing:** connected vs disconnected elements.
  - **Modality:** how "real" an image claims to be, from photographic to schematic.

  Many ad and landing-page layouts follow ideal-top/real-bottom (a lifestyle image above, price and CTA below).

**Intuition anchors**
- People recognise a visual language by its grammar, not its vocabulary. Copy the elements without the rules and you get a costume, not a language.
- If you can't name the style you're using, you're probably not using it on purpose.

**Mastery checks**
- Define a visual language's vocabulary and grammar using a real identity.
- Name ten styles and their defining codes, including three interface eras.
- Explain Osgood's semantic differential and its three dimensions, and design a 6-scale test for a logo.
- Explain Kress & van Leeuwen's given/new and ideal/real, with a landing-page example.

**Analysis**
- Choose a visual language you admire (Munich 1972, Swiss Railways, Linear, a Wes Anderson film). Extract its vocabulary (10 elements) and grammar (8 rules) into a one-page "language sheet" *[Figma: White]*. Then design one new artefact in that language and check whether a stranger attributes it correctly.

**Taste**
- Build languages, not layouts. A designer who can define a small vocabulary and a strict grammar can produce a thousand coherent artefacts. One who designs each piece from scratch produces a thousand unrelated ones.

## PART 6 — Interface Semiotics: Metaphors, States & Metacommunication [Core → Deep]

- **The interface as a message from designer to user:** Clarisse de Souza's **semiotic engineering** (*The Semiotic Engineering of Human-Computer Interaction*, 2005) frames every interface as **metacommunication**: the designer telling the user "here is what I think you want, and here is how you can do it". Unclear interfaces are *badly written messages*. It's a useful lens for critique: what is this screen saying, and is it saying it well?
- **Norman's signifier is a semiotic term:** Norman adopted "signifier" (2013) to separate what's possible (affordance) from the perceivable sign of it (signifier) (02 Part 5; `TD-01#25 · @glean_ux · DcQ0h89Bhqp`). In semiotic terms, buttons, links and handles are **signs of action**. Flat design's weakness was removing signifiers (depth, borders, underlines) until users couldn't tell text from controls. The fix is **conventional signifiers** (blue underlined links, bordered or filled buttons, the ↗ icon for "leaves this page").
- **Interface metaphors:** the **desktop metaphor** (Xerox Alto/Star, 1973–81, then Apple Lisa and Macintosh, 1983–84: desktop, files, folders, trash) made computing learnable by mapping it onto office objects. Metaphors **accelerate learning and constrain thinking**: they predict behaviour until the system does something the metaphor can't explain ("why is my 'deleted' file still in the trash?"). Modern metaphors: feeds, stories, cards, inboxes, carts, wallets, spaces. **Choose a metaphor for what it lets users predict, and watch for where it breaks.**
- **State signification:** interfaces constantly signal state: selected, disabled, loading, error, success, offline, syncing, unread, pinned. Rules:
  - **Redundant coding.** Every state is signalled by at least two channels (colour + icon/shape/text), because colour alone fails for CVD and for anyone not looking closely (WCAG 1.4.1).
  - **Consistency.** One sign per state across the product.
  - **Proportion.** Signal strength matches importance (a destructive error ≠ an info tip).
  - **Location.** The sign appears where attention is (06 Part 2).
- **Microinteractions as signs:** Dan Saffer's *Microinteractions* (2013): trigger → rules → feedback → loops and modes. Feedback motion is **indexical** (it shows the system responded) and often **iconic** (a trash can lid opening). A "like" heart's burst animation is a sign of acknowledgement. Timing and easing carry meaning too: bouncy reads as playful, linear as mechanical, slow as luxurious or sluggish (bridge to 18).
- **The AI sign problem (Frontier):** AI features needed a sign fast. The **✨ sparkle** (plus gradients, purple, "magic wand") became the convention within about two years, **a symbol learned in public in real time**. The open design problems: signalling **uncertainty** (confidence indicators, "may be inaccurate" labels), **provenance** (what was AI-generated: C2PA Content Credentials and "Made with AI" labels), and **agency** (when an agent is acting on your behalf). These are live semiotic questions you can contribute to (bridge to 29, 30). A second shift is already visible: sparkle icons, purple-and-black palettes and glassy surfaces now appear on checklists of what makes a site look machine-made (`TD-09#15 · @aj.on.ai · DcEJDHBTyPY`). A sign that means "has AI" is sliding toward a connotation of "generic", so use it for the feature, not as brand decoration.
- **Voice, tone and copy as signs:** words are the most precise signs in an interface. Button labels ("Book class" vs "Submit"), error messages and empty states communicate the brand's personality and the system's model. Full craft in `L8-design-17-microcopy-ux-writing.md`.

**Intuition anchors**
- Every screen is a message from you to the user. Reread it as a message: is it clear, honest and in their language?
- Metaphors are loans. They buy fast learning and charge interest when the system behaves unlike the thing it borrowed from.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain semiotic engineering's "metacommunication" and use it to critique one screen.
- Explain how flat design weakened signifiers and three conventional fixes.
- Explain the desktop metaphor's benefits and one place it breaks.
- State four rules for signalling UI state and apply them to a form.
- Describe the open semiotic problems of AI interfaces.

**Analysis**
- Take one AI feature (Figma AI, Gmail's "Help me write", a chatbot inside a banking app). Analyse its sign system: how it signals AI presence, uncertainty, provenance and agency. Propose a better sign set for uncertainty and provenance *[Figma: Yellow]*, and test comprehension with three people.

**Taste**
- Prefer honest, redundant, conventional signs for state and action, and save your originality for identity and delight. Users should never have to decode whether something is a button.

## PART 7 — History & Masters of Semiotics in Design [Deep]

- **Theorists:**
  - **Ferdinand de Saussure** (1916) and **C.S. Peirce** (1860s–1900s): the two founders.
  - **Charles Morris** (1938): syntactics, semantics, pragmatics.
  - **Roland Barthes:** *Mythologies* (1957), *Rhetoric of the Image* (1964), *Camera Lucida* (1980, studium and punctum in photographs: the general interest vs the detail that pierces).
  - **Umberto Eco:** *A Theory of Semiotics* (1976), and the concept of the "open work".
  - **Stuart Hall:** encoding/decoding (1973).
  - **Judith Williamson:** *Decoding Advertisements* (1978), how ads transfer meaning from signs to products.
  - **John Berger:** *Ways of Seeing* (BBC and book, 1972), the gaze, publicity images and the ideology of looking. Essential and short.
  - **Marshall McLuhan:** *Understanding Media* (1964), "the medium is the message".
  - **Kress & van Leeuwen:** *Reading Images* (1996), the grammar of visual design.
  - **Jean Baudrillard:** *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981), signs referring only to other signs, uncannily relevant to AI imagery.
- **Indian traditions of codified signs:**
  - **Bharata's *Nāṭyaśāstra*** (c. 200 BCE–200 CE), with its theory of **rasa** (aesthetic emotion) and codified **mudras** (hand gestures) and **abhinaya** (expression): one of the world's oldest systematic sign languages for performance.
  - **Iconometry** in temple sculpture (Shilpa Shastras: proportions and attributes that identify deities: vahanas, weapons, gestures). Iconography as a strict visual code centuries before corporate identity manuals.
  - **Folk and vernacular sign systems:** Warli's circles, triangles and lines (`TD-12#11 · @chirovsduniya · DTeR8rIE3G-`); Bengali alpana grammar (`TD-14#2 · @sahana.144 · DcU999IIw_T`); matchbox labels (`TD-14#9 · @eyes_of_apoorva · DaAnRP-NhSg`); truck art and calendar art (`TD-07#36 · @pritamkrox`).
  - **Chaturanga** as the ancestor of chess's sign system: pieces as codified representations of the four divisions of an army (`TD-14#10 · @tallstoreys · DUoz4nnkvJX`).
- **Designers who worked semiotically:** Otto Neurath (Isotype), Otl Aicher (pictograms as a language), Paul Rand (rebus, wit), Massimo Vignelli (semantics/syntactics/pragmatics), Milton Glaser (symbolic compression), Susan Kare (the original Macintosh icons, 1984: making computing legible through small, witty, pixel-constrained signs), Pentagram (identities as sign systems), Sagmeister (meaning through material and body), D. Udaya Kumar (the ₹ sign, 2010: a national symbol built from two scripts).
- **Researchers of how signs are actually read:** Siné McDougall (icon characteristics and semantic distance), Indrani Medhi (text-free interfaces for low-literacy users, Microsoft Research India), and the ISO 9186 committee's comprehension-testing method. They turned "is it clear?" into something you can measure.

**Intuition anchors**
- The best designers are fluent readers of culture first. Semiotic theory gives the vocabulary, and a lifetime of noticing gives the fluency.

**Mastery checks**
- Name eight theorists and one idea each usable in critique.
- Explain Barthes' studium and punctum with a photograph.
- Explain how the *Nāṭyaśāstra*'s mudras function as a codified sign system.
- Explain Susan Kare's contribution in semiotic terms.

**Analysis**
- Watch *Ways of Seeing* episode 1 (widely available). Write a one-page application of Berger's argument to a current Indian ad campaign or Instagram brand feed.

**Taste**
- Read the theorists for tools, not jargon. The goal is a sharper eye and clearer critique, not quoting Barthes in a client meeting.

## PART 8 — Applied Semiotics: Analysis, Positioning & Testing [Core → Deep]

- **The semiotic analysis method, for any artefact:**
  1. **Inventory:** list every sign (image, colour, type, layout, material, copy, motion, sound).
  2. **Denotation:** what each literally is.
  3. **Connotation:** what each suggests, and to whom.
  4. **Codes:** which category, genre and cultural codes it uses or breaks.
  5. **Myth:** what naturalised belief it relies on.
  6. **Readings:** dominant, negotiated and oppositional audiences.
  7. **Coherence:** do the signs agree, or does one contradict the rest?
  8. **Verdict:** does the meaning serve the brief?

  Use it for critique, competitor audits and your own work before presenting.
- **Semiotics in brand positioning:** audit the category (residual/dominant/emergent codes), choose which codes to borrow (for recognition) and which to break (for distinctiveness), then **specify the brand's code**: which signs it owns (a colour, a shape, a phrase, a sound) and which it avoids. A narrative product name creates an obligation the design must meet (`TD-12#12 · @udeatdesign · DSgHFvHkqxK`: "does the design still tell the story?"). Build a period study to see codes clearly by translating a brand into another decade (`TD-06#21 · @kxdgraphics · DFQuBwZAVqq`). Full strategy in 10.
- **Anticipating misreadings:** before launch, list plausible unintended readings: cultural (gestures, colours, animals, religious symbols), political (colours and symbols with party associations in India), sexual or crude (accidental shapes in logos, the famous failure category), translation failures (brand names in other languages), and meme potential. **Show the work to people outside your bubble.**
- **Testing meaning:**
  - **Icon comprehension tests** (ISO 9186-style, or a simple version: show the icon without a label and ask what it means, scoring correct, partial or wrong).
  - **Semantic differential** ratings against a target profile.
  - **5-second "what is this?" tests.**
  - **Word-association tests** (show the logo, collect three words).
  - **Card sorting** for category signals ("which of these looks like a bank?").
  - **Cross-cultural panels.**

  Every test is cheap. Skipping them is how brands launch with meanings nobody intended.
- **Designing for multiple readers at once:** India contains many codes (urban/rural, regional languages, religious communities, generations). Strategies: layered meaning (a sign that works on multiple levels), redundancy (icon + word + colour), local variants within a global system, and deliberate neutrality where offence is costly (government, health, emergency).

**Intuition anchors**
- If you haven't imagined how someone could misread your design, someone will do it for you in public.
- Meaning is testable. "I think it reads as premium" is a hypothesis. Ten ratings make it evidence.

**Mastery checks**
- Run the eight-step semiotic analysis on one artefact, from memory.
- Explain how to position a brand by borrowing and breaking category codes.
- List five categories of misreading to check before launch.
- Design an icon comprehension test and a semantic-differential test.

**Analysis**
- Run a full semiotic analysis of your own EcoTrace (or Meridian UI) landing page: all eight steps, plus a semantic-differential test with 10 people on 6 scales against your intended profile. Report the gaps and three changes.

**Taste**
- Make meaning deliberately and verify it humbly. Great identities feel inevitable because their makers analysed codes ruthlessly and tested readings with people who weren't in the room.

## PART 9 — How Real Users Read Signs: Comprehension, Misreading & Evidence [Core → Deep]

- **Three different questions hide inside "is this icon understood?"**
  - **Guessability:** can a first-time user say what it means with no label? ISO 9186-style tests measure this.
  - **Learnability:** once told, do they get it right next time?
  - **Memorability:** do they still get it right a week later, or after months away?

  Public signs need guessability, because nobody will teach the reader. Product icons that ship with labels mostly need learnability and memorability. Decide which one you're testing before you test.
- **What drives comprehension.** Siné McDougall, Martin Curry and Oscar de Bruijn (1999) rated 239 symbols on **concreteness** (does it depict a real object?), **visual complexity**, **familiarity**, **meaningfulness** and **semantic distance** (how far the picture is from the function). A printer for "print" is close. A gear for "settings" is further. A sparkle for "AI" is very far. Their later work suggests concreteness helps most on first encounter and **familiarity takes over with use**. **Design consequence:** a new function gets either a close, concrete picture or a label. A distant symbol works only once repetition has made it familiar.
- **Labels win when learning matters.** Susan Wiedenbeck (1999) found icon-only interfaces the hardest to learn, compared with labels or icons with labels. NN/g's 2016 study of hidden navigation found that menus behind a hamburger icon were used less and discovered less, most sharply on desktop. Tooltips don't rescue unlabelled icons on touch screens, because there's no hover.
- **A typology of real misreadings** (use it to label failures in tests):
  - **Wrong referent:** the object is recognised but points to the wrong function. A cloud read as "upload" when it means "synced"; a bookmark read as "download".
  - **Platform clash:** iOS share (a box with an arrow) and Android share (three connected dots) each mean nothing to users of the other platform.
  - **Dead metaphor:** the floppy disk, the phone handset (Part 1).
  - **Missing referent:** the US mailbox, the cheque book for users who've only used UPI.
  - **Inverted code:** a ✓ that marks an error in some traditions (Part 2), red for "up" in Chinese markets (03 Part 4).
  - **Over-reading:** a user infers a promise the sign doesn't make, such as a padlock read as "this site is safe" (Part 11).
- **Low-literacy and first-generation users.** Indrani Medhi and colleagues at Microsoft Research India built text-free interfaces for low-literacy users in Bangalore in the mid-2000s. Abstract icons that literate designers find obvious weren't self-explanatory; richer, more concrete imagery and consistent voice help did far better. Don't generalise this into "Indian users need pictures". EcoTrace's accountants are fluent readers. But a supplier onboarding flow that reaches small workshops through a WhatsApp link meets a different reader, and must be tested with that reader.
- **Worked decision: EcoTrace's navigation.**
  - **Items:** Dashboard, Data collection, Emissions (Scope 1, 2, 3), BRSR report, Suppliers, Evidence, Settings.
  - **Options:** (A) an icon-only rail (compact); (B) icon plus label, always; (C) labelled by default, collapsible to an icon rail by the user's choice.
  - **Criteria:** semantic distance ("Scope 3" and "BRSR" have no conventional picture); frequency of use (accountants daily, owners monthly, and monthly users forget); and screen width (budget office laptops at 1366 px wide are still common, so every pixel of sidebar counts).
  - **Choice:** C. Owners and new users get labels; daily users who've learned the icons can reclaim space.
  - **Consequences and checks:** log how many users collapse the rail and when. Run a memorability test on the collapsed rail with users who've had it for a month. If "Evidence" and "Data collection" get confused, the icons are too close in meaning and one needs redrawing.
- **How to read a comprehension test.** Overall "% correct" hides the useful information. Build a **confusion matrix**: rows are the intended meanings, columns are what people answered, and the off-diagonal cells show which signs are being mistaken for which. Two icons that swap meanings need redrawing apart. An icon that attracts random answers needs a label. Score answers with two raters against a written rubric (correct, partly correct, wrong), as ISO 9186 procedures do, so your hopes don't grade the test.

**Intuition anchors**
- Guessable, learnable and memorable are three different targets. Product icons with labels only need the last two.
- The confusion matrix tells you what to fix; the percentage only tells you that something's wrong.

**Mastery checks**
- Distinguish guessability, learnability and memorability, and say which a hospital sign and a toolbar icon each need.
- Define semantic distance and rank five icons by it.
- Classify six real misreadings using the typology.
- Explain why a confusion matrix beats a percentage-correct score.

**Analysis**
- Run a two-stage test on EcoTrace's (or any SaaS product's) sidebar icons: an unlabelled guessability round with eight people, then a labelled learning round and an unlabelled recall round two days later. Build the confusion matrix, classify each misreading, and redesign the two worst icons *[Figma: Yellow]*.

**Taste**
- Treat every unlabelled icon as a claim that users already know it, and ask for the evidence. Most icons you're proud of are words your users haven't learned yet.

## PART 10 — Meaning in India: Politics, Religion, Language, Law & Everyday Codes [Core → Deep]

- **India isn't one reader.** The Eighth Schedule of the Constitution lists 22 languages, written in many scripts, and religious, regional, caste, class and generational codes overlap in every audience. A sign that's neutral in one reading position can be loaded in the next. This Part maps the codes most likely to catch a product designer out.
- **Political symbols are pictograms for voters.** The Election Commission allots each party a symbol so that voters who can't read can still find their candidate on the ballot: the lotus (BJP), the hand (Congress), the broom (AAP), the elephant (BSP), the bicycle (Samajwadi Party). It's Isotype's logic (Part 4) at national scale, and it loads these everyday objects. The lotus is also the national flower and a Hindu and Buddhist sacred symbol, so a lotus in a brand mark has several readings at once. **Rule:** before using any common object as a mark, check whether a party owns it in the regions you sell in.
- **Colour carries politics and faith.** Saffron, green and blue (associated with Ambedkarite and Dalit movements) all carry political and religious readings alongside their general ones (03 Part 4). The tricolour's display is governed by the Flag Code of India, 2002, and the State Emblem of India (Prohibition of Improper Use) Act, 2005 restricts use of the Lion Capital emblem. **Design consequence:** a private product that borrows government insignia or tricolour gradients to look official is using a restricted code and the same trick scam sites use. EcoTrace earns regulatory credibility by citing SEBI's requirements accurately, never by looking like an MCA or SEBI portal.
- **Sacred signs in commerce.** The svastika and *Shubh Labh* appear in traditional account books opened at Diwali (Chopda Pujan in Gujarati trading communities), Ganesha appears on shop signs and invoices, and the kalash and Om mark beginnings. They're familiar and warm to many users and meaningless or unwelcome to others. For a product used by businesses of every faith, keep them out of the interface and leave festive greetings to opt-in, user-chosen themes.
- **Oppositional readings happen in public.** Tanishq withdrew a 2020 advertisement showing an interfaith baby shower after an online backlash, and Fabindia withdrew a 2021 Diwali collection name ("Jashn-e-Riwaaz") after similar objections. Whatever one thinks of the campaigns, the semiotic lesson is Hall's (Part 2): the brand encoded harmony, and a vocal audience decoded something else. **You can't control reading positions, but you can map them before launch** and decide in advance whether you'll stand behind the work.
- **Food marks: a legal sign system with redundancy.** FSSAI's vegetarian mark is a green circle inside a green square. Its 2020 labelling regulations made the non-vegetarian mark a **brown triangle** inside a square, so the two differ in shape as well as colour, which helps colour-blind readers. Food-delivery apps reuse these marks as shared vocabulary. When Zomato announced a "pure veg" delivery fleet in green uniforms in 2024, it dropped the green uniforms within a day after objections: colour-coding *people* by diet read as marking them by purity, a far heavier meaning than the one intended.
- **Direction-of-money signs: the UPI collect-request scam.** A UPI "collect request" is a payee asking a payer to approve a payment. Fraudsters send one framed as "You've received ₹5,000, enter your PIN to accept". The victim's mental model ("PIN to receive") beats the screen's signs. The semiotic fix is to make direction unmistakable: "You will **pay** ₹5,000 to [name]", an outgoing-arrow icon, the payee's verified name, and the rule banks and NPCI repeat: **you never enter your UPI PIN to receive money**. Any EcoTrace payment or refund screen should state direction in words, not rely on colour or arrows alone.
- **Scripts, numbers and mirroring.** Urdu is written right to left, so an Urdu interface mirrors directional icons (back arrows, progress direction) but not clocks or media playback controls, following platform bidirectionality guidance. Labels in Tamil or Malayalam often run much longer than their English source, so icon-plus-label layouts need room to grow (17 Part 5). **The ₹ sign** (designed by D. Udaya Kumar and adopted in 2010, blending Devanagari र and Latin R) replaces the $ that most icon sets ship as their "money" default, and Indian digit grouping (1,23,456) goes with it.
- **Worked decision: EcoTrace's colour code.**
  - **Options:** (A) the green-and-leaf eco code; (B) a navy and teal "financial reporting" code, with green kept for success states only; (C) a saffron accent for warmth.
  - **Criteria:** category cliché (leaves and green gradients are the greenwashing code, Part 12 anti-taste); state clarity (if the brand is green, "success green" stops standing out); political and religious readings of saffron and green; and the buyer, who is a CFO or compliance lead, not a consumer.
  - **Choice:** B. The environmental meaning is carried by the content (real emissions data), not by decoration.
  - **Consequences:** the product reads as "serious reporting tool" first and "eco" second, which is the right order for a legal filing. A 5-second test should show strangers guessing "finance or compliance software", not "a gardening app".

**Intuition anchors**
- In India, assume every common object, colour and gesture already belongs to someone: a party, a faith, a region or a law. Check before you borrow it.
- Direction, money and identity are where misreadings cost the most. Say them in words.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain why party symbols exist and name five, with one design consequence.
- Name two Indian laws that restrict the use of national symbols, and what they mean for a product.
- Explain the FSSAI marks as redundant coding and the Zomato uniform episode as a connotation failure.
- Explain the UPI collect-request scam in semiotic terms and design the fix.
- Name two icon types that mirror in an RTL interface and two that don't.

**Analysis**
- Take one festive campaign and one fintech payment screen from Indian brands. For each, map at least four reading positions (by faith, region, generation and digital familiarity), predict each reading, then ask one person from each of two positions. Record where your predictions failed.

**Taste**
- Design for the whole country without flattening it: keep product signs neutral and exact, and let users choose cultural warmth (festive themes, languages, greetings) rather than having it chosen for them.

## PART 11 — Signs of Trust, Status & Provenance: Worked Decisions for EcoTrace & Meridian UI [Deep]

- **A trust sign is worth only the process behind it.** When the process weakens, the sign keeps its old meaning in users' heads for years, and that gap is where misreading and fraud live.
  - **The browser padlock** meant "this connection is encrypted", but users read it as "this site is trustworthy", and phishing sites could get certificates easily. In 2023 Google replaced it in Chrome with a neutral settings icon, citing research that few users understood what the padlock meant.
  - **The blue verification tick** on Twitter meant "identity checked" until 2022, when it became available by subscription. Impersonation followed within days, including a fake pharmaceutical-company account whose tweet coincided with a fall in the real company's share price. The sign had turned from an index of verification into a symbol of payment.
- **Certification marks are law as well as graphics.** The ISI mark and BIS hallmark mean something because an institution audits the claim, and the Trade Marks Act, 1999 provides for certification marks as a category (10 Part 7). **EcoTrace can't invent a "BRSR Certified" badge**: nothing stands behind it, and it would mislead in exactly the way consumer law targets (06 Part 6).
- **Worked decision: EcoTrace's disclosure status system.** Each BRSR section moves through Not started → In progress → Needs attention → Ready for review → Filed, with Assured added when a third-party provider has assured the data.
  - **Options:** (A) traffic lights (red, amber, green); (B) neutral states with icons and words, using colour only for exceptions; (C) a percentage ring per section.
  - **Criteria:** redundant coding (06 Part 2, WCAG 1.4.1); emotional valence, since a first-day dashboard full of red reads as "you're already non-compliant" and makes owners panic or leave (06 Part 5); honesty of each word, since "Assured" is a professional term with a specific meaning; and distinctness in greyscale.
  - **Choice:** B. "Not started" is grey, not red, because nothing is wrong yet. "Needs attention" is amber with a "!" and the reason in words ("2 electricity bills missing"). "Ready for review" is a check. "Filed" shows the date and who filed, an index rather than a colour. "Assured" appears only when the provider's report is attached. The percentage ring was rejected because 90% complete says nothing about which 10% is missing.
  - **Consequences:** less dramatic dashboards, fewer panicked support calls, and states that auditors can take literally.
- **Provenance beats badges.** "Verified" says nothing about what was checked. "Source: MSEDCL electricity bill, March 2026, uploaded by R. Sharma, 4 April" is an index the user or auditor can follow. Pair it with a data-quality tag whose **shape** differs as well as its colour (measured ●, calculated ◆, estimated ○), so the meaning survives printing and colour blindness (06 Part 10 on false precision).
- **Worked decision: Meridian UI encodes meaning rules in its API** (11 Part 10; 14 Part 3).
  - **`IconButton` requires a `label` prop** (a TypeScript error without it), used as the accessible name and the tooltip (15 Part 3).
  - **An allowlist of icons that may appear without visible text**: close, search, more, chevrons, play and pause. Every entry is backed by a comprehension test logged in the docs. Any other icon in an icon-only button triggers a development warning.
  - **`Badge` takes a `tone`** (neutral, info, success, warning, danger) and requires either text or an icon with text, so tone can never be colour alone.
  - **Options rejected:** documentation-only rules (broken within a month) and banning icon-only buttons entirely (wasteful for the universal few).
  - **Consequence:** the semiotic policy is enforced where products are built, and changing the allowlist becomes a reviewed decision with evidence, not a matter of taste.
- **Greenwashing is a sign problem.** Leaves, globes, green gradients and "eco" seals signal virtue without evidence. ASCI's code already requires advertising claims to be substantiated. For a product whose value is honest environmental data, **the only credible eco sign is a traceable number**.

**Intuition anchors**
- If you can't point to the process behind a trust sign, it's decoration, and users will eventually find out.
- Provenance is the honest trust sign: say where the number came from, who touched it and when.

**Mastery checks**
- Explain the padlock and blue-tick cases as index-to-symbol shifts.
- Explain why a product can't create its own certification badge, with the legal and semiotic reasons.
- Defend EcoTrace's status system against a traffic-light alternative on four criteria.
- Describe three Meridian UI API rules that enforce meaning, and the risk each prevents.

**Analysis**
- Audit the trust signs on three Indian B2B SaaS landing pages and one government portal: badges, seals, ticks, logos of clients, "secure" claims. For each, name the process behind it (or its absence), classify it as index or decoration, and redesign one page's trust section around provenance *[Figma: Yellow]*.

**Taste**
- Make fewer, truer claims. A product that shows its sources needs no seals, and one that needs seals usually has no sources.

## PART 12 — Connections & Taste Development [Lifelong]

- **What good semiotic work looks like:** every sign agrees with the others (coherent connotation); category codes are used knowingly (borrowed for recognition, broken for distinction); symbols are labelled until truly learned; state is redundantly coded; metaphors are chosen for prediction and bounded; cultural and misreading risks are checked; rhetoric carries one idea with minimal means; and the meaning was tested, not assumed.
- **Voices and sources worth internalising:**
  - **John Berger**, *Ways of Seeing*: the gaze and publicity.
  - **Roland Barthes**, *Mythologies* and *Rhetoric of the Image*: connotation and myth.
  - **Kress & van Leeuwen**, *Reading Images*: a grammar you can apply tomorrow.
  - **Judith Williamson**, *Decoding Advertisements*.
  - **Daniel Chandler**, *Semiotics: The Basics*: the clearest introduction.
  - **Sean Hall**, *This Means This, This Means That*: a visual primer for designers.
  - **Ellen Lupton & J. Abbott Miller**, *Design Writing Research*: semiotics applied to graphic design.
  - **Clarisse de Souza** (semiotic engineering), for interfaces.
- **Deliberate taste exercises:**
  1. **Ad of the day:** denotation, connotation, myth in five minutes.
  2. **Code map monthly:** one category's residual/dominant/emergent codes.
  3. **Icon comprehension test** on every icon set you make.
  4. **Style naming:** coin one style name a week for something you saw.
  5. **Cross-cultural check:** one international brand's local adaptation per month (McDonald's India menu boards, Apple's Diwali campaigns).
  6. **Sign-system study:** one system per month (mudras, railway signage, emoji, flags, hallmarks).
- **Anti-taste:** unlabelled symbolic icons, colour-only state, dead metaphors kept for nostalgia, stock-photo codes ("diverse team laughing at laptop"), category clichés adopted unexamined (blue fintech, leaf eco), exoticist "Indian" styling (Samarkan, random mandalas), symbols used without knowing their cultural weight, rhetoric with three ideas, and faked indices (fake progress, fake viewers).
- **Connections:**
  - **→ 03:** colour connotation.
  - **→ 04:** typographic register.
